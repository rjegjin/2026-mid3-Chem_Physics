Lecture 07: $d$-Orbital Splitting, CFSE, and the Spin-State Competition

0. What This Model Is, and What It Is Not

Crystal field theory treats every ligand as a negative point charge. There are no bonds, no orbital overlap, no covalency. Taken literally it is false. It also predicts the colours and magnetic moments of thousands of complexes.

What it gets right

  • ● The pattern of $d$-orbital splitting in any geometry — because that follows from symmetry alone (Modules 01–02), not from the bonding model.
  • ● Which complexes are high spin and which are low spin.
  • ● Magnetic moments, to a first approximation.
  • ● Trends in hydration enthalpy and ionic radius across a period.
What it cannot do. It cannot explain the spectrochemical series. If ligands were point charges, $\mathrm{OH^-}$ (charged) would split more strongly than $\mathrm{NH_3}$ (neutral). It does not — the order is close to the reverse. A purely electrostatic model has no mechanism for this, and no amount of parameter fitting will supply one.

That failure is not a footnote. It is the reason Module 08 exists.

Prerequisite from Module 04

Every diagram here assumes the metal centre is a $d^{\,n}$ ion with no valence $s$ electrons — that $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ is $[\mathrm{Ar}]3d^6$ and not $[\mathrm{Ar}]3d^44s^2$. Module 04 §11 established this. Without it, "$d^{\,n}$" would be an assumption rather than a fact.

1. Where the Splitting Comes From

Bring six negative charges up along $\pm x, \pm y, \pm z$. An electron in a $d$ orbital that points at a ligand is repelled more than one pointing between ligands.

$$ d \;\longrightarrow\; t_{2g} + e_g \qquad (O_h) $$
SetOrbitalsOrientationEnergy
$e_g$$d_{z^2},\ d_{x^2-y^2}$along the axes$+0.6\,\Delta_o$
$t_{2g}$$d_{xy},\ d_{yz},\ d_{zx}$between the axes$-0.4\,\Delta_o$
Why is $d_{z^2}$ in $e_g$? From the picture, $d_{z^2}$ aims along one axis while $d_{x^2-y^2}$ aims along four, so their being degenerate looks wrong. The resolution is that $d_{z^2}$ is really the sum of $d_{z^2-x^2}$ and $d_{z^2-y^2}$ — six such functions exist but only five are independent. That symmetry groups the two together is something the character tables of Module 02 already told us.

2. The Barycentre Rule

Why $+0.6$ and $-0.4$? The ligand field raises the average energy of the whole $d$ set; relative to that average, the redistribution must sum to zero.

$$ 2\,(+0.6\,\Delta_o) + 3\,(-0.4\,\Delta_o) = 1.2 - 1.2 = 0 $$
Definition

$\Delta_o \equiv E(e_g) - E(t_{2g})$. Spectroscopists also write it $10\,Dq$ — the same quantity under another name.

Caution

The barycentre is the spherical-field reference, already raised above the free ion. A negative CFSE does not mean the complex is more stable than the free ion.

Magnitude

$\Delta_o$ is typically $10\,000$–$30\,000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ — the visible range. That is why these complexes are coloured.

Bethe, 1929. Hans Bethe derived this splitting by treating the electrostatic field around an ion in a crystal group-theoretically — without discussing chemical bonding at all. It was a physicist's result, and inorganic chemistry took almost twenty years to notice it.

3. Other Geometries

Tetrahedral — the labels invert

$$ \Delta_t \approx \tfrac{4}{9}\,\Delta_o $$
  • ● $e$ ($d_{z^2}, d_{x^2-y^2}$) now lies below $t_2$ ($d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{zx}$)
  • ● No $d$ orbital points straight at a ligand — $t_2$, aiming between the axes, comes closest
  • ● There is no $g$ subscript: $T_d$ has no centre of inversion. That becomes decisive for the Laporte rule in Module 09
$4/9$ is an estimate, not a derivation. Six ligands become four, giving $\tfrac{2}{3}$; the orientation is worse, giving another $\tfrac{2}{3}$; multiply for $\tfrac{4}{9}$. It is hand-waving inside a point-charge model, and measured values scatter about it.

Square planar — the limit with $z$ removed

Take the octahedron and move the two $z$-axis ligands to infinity. Orbitals with $z$ character ($d_{z^2}, d_{xz}, d_{yz}$) fall; those in the $xy$ plane ($d_{xy}, d_{x^2-y^2}$) rise.

OrbitalEnergy / $\Delta_o$

The order of $d_{z^2}$ and $d_{xy}$ is not universal. These are the standard point-charge values; in real complexes the two can swap depending on the ligand. What is robust is that $d_{x^2-y^2}$ sits far above everything else — and that is what makes square-planar $d^8$ diamagnetic.

5. Crystal Field Stabilisation Energy

$$ \mathrm{CFSE} = \left(-0.4\,n_{t_{2g}} + 0.6\,n_{e_g}\right)\Delta_o \;+\; m\,P $$

$m$ is the number of pairs formed beyond those the free ion already had. The $n-5$ pairs that are unavoidable for $n>5$ are not counted — they are not the ligand field's doing.

Low-spin $d^4$ is $t_{2g}^4$ and has two unpaired electrons — not zero. "Low spin" does not mean "pair up as much as possible"; it means crowd into the lower level, and within that level Hund's rule still operates: $t_{2g}^4 = \uparrow\downarrow\ \uparrow\ \uparrow$. 6. Where CFSE Shows Up in Data

CFSE is a small fraction of the total bonding energy. It does not give absolute values; it explains trends. Two of them are visible.

Hydration enthalpies, $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}} \to \mathrm{Zn^{2+}}$. Schematic.

Why two humps

The straight line is the monotonic rise from increasing $Z_{\text{eff}}$. CFSE is superimposed on it. For high-spin $d^0$, $d^5$ and $d^{10}$ the CFSE is exactly zero, so only those three points lie on the line and everything between sags below it (more stable). There are two humps because CFSE returns to zero at $d^5$.

Ionic radii do the same thing

While $t_{2g}$ is filling, the electrons avoid the ligands and the radius is smaller than expected; filling $e_g$ pushes the ligands away and enlarges it. That is why radii do not shrink smoothly across the period.

Avoid the circular argument. Explaining this curve by CFSE and then proving CFSE from this curve demonstrates nothing. The independent evidence is spectroscopic — reading $\Delta_o$ directly off an absorption band. That is Module 09.

7. High Spin and Low Spin — Two Energies Competing

$$ \Delta_o \;\gtrless\; P $$

$\Delta_o < P$ → high spin

Promotion is cheaper than pairing. Electrons climb into $e_g$ and stay unpaired. A weak field.

$\mathrm{[Fe(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}$ — $d^6$ high spin, four unpaired electrons, paramagnetic.

$\Delta_o > P$ → low spin

Promotion is expensive. Electrons crowd into $t_{2g}$ and pair up. A strong field.

$\mathrm{[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}}$ — the same $d^6$, now low spin, no unpaired electrons, diamagnetic.

What is inside $P$

The pairing energy has two parts — the Coulomb repulsion of two electrons in one orbital, and the exchange stabilisation that is lost when parallel spins are given up. The second is the very term used in Module 04 §9 to justify Hund's rule. So the high-spin/low-spin competition is a fight between the ligand field and Hund's rule.

A choice exists only for $d^4$–$d^7$. For $d^1$–$d^3$ there is still an empty $t_{2g}$ orbital, so nothing forces pairing; for $d^8$–$d^{10}$ the $t_{2g}$ set is already full and the electrons have nowhere to go but $e_g$. That is why the spin buttons in the applet switch off outside that window — in octahedral geometry.

The practical consequence of row two

Because $\Delta$ is large for $4d$ and $5d$ metals, they are almost always low spin. The diamagnetic square-planar $d^8$ of $\mathrm{Pd^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Pt^{2+}}$ follows from this, and it is one reason the organometallic chemistry of Modules 11–13 leans so heavily on $4d$ and $5d$ metals.

Row three is where this model ends. On a point-charge picture, charged $\mathrm{OH^-}$ should split more strongly than neutral $\mathrm{NH_3}$. The observation is the opposite. Ligand order is set not by electrostatics but by orbital overlap — $\sigma$ donation, $\pi$ donation, $\pi$ acceptance. Module 08 derives the series as a conclusion rather than a list to memorise.

Why this is useful

The magnetic moment is measured. Put the measurement into this expression and it returns the number of unpaired electrons, which settles whether the complex is high or low spin. Magnetic measurement therefore adjudicates the competition of §7 experimentally.

Why "spin-only". The formula sets the orbital contribution to zero. Because the ligand field displaces $L_z$ from being a good quantum number ($\text{quenching}$), that assumption largely holds for 3$d$ metals. It does not hold for $T$ ground terms, nor for 4$d$, 5$d$ and the lanthanides. When and why it fails is Module 10.

10. Jahn–Teller Distortion

A non-linear molecule in a degenerate electronic ground state must distort so as to remove that degeneracy.

ConfigurationDegeneracy inDistortion
$d^9$$e_g^3$large
$d^4$ high spin$e_g^1$large
$d^7$ low spin$e_g^1$large
$d^1, d^2$$t_{2g}$small
$d^3$, $d^6$ low spinnonenone

Why $e_g$ is strong and $t_{2g}$ weak

$e_g$ points straight at the ligands, so a small change in bond length moves its energy a great deal. $t_{2g}$ points between them and responds far less to the same distortion.

$\mathrm{[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}$ — a $d^9$ ion. The four equatorial bonds are about 1.95 Å; the two axial ones about 2.4 Å, conspicuously longer.

Jahn & Teller, 1937. Hermann Jahn and Edward Teller proved this not by experiment but by group theory — showing that in every point group except the linear ones there exists an antisymmetric vibrational mode capable of removing an electronic degeneracy. The theorem says only that a distortion occurs; it says nothing about how large.

11. The Working Life of This Model

Bethe, 1929

Derived the level splitting of an ion in a crystal group-theoretically. A result from physics, and one that did not reach chemistry directly.

Van Vleck, 1932–35

Applied it to magnetic data and made crystal field theory a chemical tool. He also pointed out the limits of a purely electrostatic model himself and proposed a treatment mixing in orbital overlap — which became ligand field theory. Nobel Prize in Physics, 1977.

The 1950s

Orgel, Tanabe and Sugano succeeded in interpreting electronic spectra within the crystal field framework. A wrong model gets the spectra right because the pattern of the splitting comes from symmetry.

One sentence to carry out of this module

Symmetry fixes the pattern of the splitting; bonding fixes its size. Crystal field theory holds only the first half.

So the next module is not a new topic but the other half of the same picture. Module 08 keeps the $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ labels intact while re-deriving them from molecular orbitals rather than point charges — and in doing so explains why the size of $\Delta_o$ depends on the ligand.
