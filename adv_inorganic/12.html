0. Scope

Central question

What elementary steps are available to a metal complex?

Requires

Module 11 — electron counting

Feeds

Module 13 — catalytic cycles are combinations of these steps

One analytical frame throughout. Every reaction is tracked simultaneously in oxidation state, electron count and coordination number. Each section's table uses those same three columns.

1. Ligand Substitution

$$ \mathrm{ML}_n + \mathrm{L}' \longrightarrow \mathrm{ML}_{n-1}\mathrm{L}' + \mathrm{L} $$
  • Dissociative — the usual route for 18-electron complexes; rate independent of the incoming ligand
  • Associative — the usual route for 16-electron square-planar $d^8$
  • Interchange ($\mathrm{I_a}$, $\mathrm{I_d}$) between the two extremes
  • The trans effect — a synthetic tool in platinum(II) chemistry
  • Oxidation state, electron count and coordination number all unchanged

2. Oxidative Addition

$$ \mathrm{M}^{n} + \mathrm{X}\text{--}\mathrm{Y} \longrightarrow \mathrm{X}\text{--}\mathrm{M}^{n+2}\text{--}\mathrm{Y} $$
  • Oxidation state +2, electron count +2, coordination number +2
  • Requirements — an electron-rich metal, 16 electrons or fewer, a vacant site
  • Concerted — cis products. $\mathrm{H_2}$, C–H bonds
  • $\mathrm{S_N2}$-like — trans products. Alkyl halides
  • Radical pathways
  • Vaska's complex $\mathrm{IrCl(CO)(PPh_3)_2}$ as the reference case

3. Reductive Elimination

  • Exactly the reverse — oxidation state, electron count and coordination number each −2
  • The two groups must be cis. That single constraint governs catalyst design
  • Promoted by bulky ligands, an electron-poor metal, or oxidation
  • Where the C–C bond is made — the last step of every cross-coupling

4. Migratory Insertion

$$ \mathrm{M}\text{--}\mathrm{R} + \mathrm{CO} \longrightarrow \mathrm{M}\text{--}\mathrm{C(O)}\mathrm{R} $$
  • Oxidation state unchanged, electron count −2, coordination number −1 → a site opens
  • It is the alkyl that migrates, not the CO that inserts — established by isotopic labelling
  • Alkene insertion — the chain-growth step of polymerisation and hydroformylation
  • The reverse reaction — de-insertion (extrusion)

5. $\beta$-Hydride Elimination

$$ \mathrm{M}\text{--}\mathrm{CH_2CH_2R} \longrightarrow \mathrm{M(H)}(\eta^2\text{-}\mathrm{CH_2{=}CHR}) $$
  • Oxidation state unchanged, electron count +2, coordination number +1
  • Requires a $\beta$ hydrogen, a vacant site, and a near-coplanar M–C–C–H unit
  • In most catalysts this is the unwanted decomposition path
  • Suppressing it — groups with no $\beta$-H (methyl, neopentyl, aryl), or saturating the coordination sphere
  • Exploiting it — chain transfer, alkene isomerisation

6. Electron Transfer

  • Outer sphere — no bond is broken and no ligand is shared. Marcus theory: the rate is set by the reorganisation energy $\lambda$, not by any new bond
  • Inner sphere — the electron crosses a bridging ligand. Taube's chloride-labelling experiment (1953) settled that the bridge is real
  • Why self-exchange rates span twelve orders of magnitude: how far the bond lengths must move before the electron can hop
  • The inverted region — a more favourable $\Delta G$ making the reaction slower. Marcus predicted it in 1956; Miller and Closs measured it in 1984
  • Link back to Module 07 — high spin versus low spin decides how far the metal–ligand bonds move on reduction, so it decides $\lambda$. Co(III)/Co(II) self-exchange is slow for exactly this reason

7. Key Competencies

  • Distinguish the five steps by their changes in oxidation state, electron count and coordination number
  • Judge from the electron count which steps a given complex can undergo
  • State the conditions for $\beta$-H elimination and how to suppress it
  • Give the conditions under which any of these steps runs in reverse
