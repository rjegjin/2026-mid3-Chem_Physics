Lecture 09 (two sessions): Microstates, Term Symbols, and Tanabe–Sugano Analysis

0. Scope

Central question

Where does the absorption spectrum of a transition-metal complex come from?

Modules 07 and 08 produced one number, $\Delta_o$, and treated the $d$ electrons as if they only ever sat in orbitals. A spectrum is not a property of orbitals. It is a set of differences between states of the whole $d^{\,n}$ system, and those states are built from electron–electron repulsion as much as from the ligand field. That is why this module needs its own vocabulary before it can read a single band.

Requires

Module 04 — Slater determinants and angular momentum · Modules 07–08 — $\Delta_o$ and its ligand dependence

Feeds

Module 10 — spin–orbit coupling and magnetism · Module 15 Case 4 — luminescent complexes

Two sessions are allotted to this module. Spin–orbit coupling is deliberately excluded and deferred to Module 10; everything here is non-relativistic, and every state carries a good $S$. The break falls after §7 — the first session builds the states, the second reads the spectrum.

1. What Is Actually Measured

Before any theory: a UV–visible spectrum is a plot of molar absorptivity $\varepsilon$ against wavenumber, and it carries exactly three pieces of information per band.

Position

$\tilde\nu$ in $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ — an energy difference between two states of the complex. This is what the theory has to predict.

Intensity

$\varepsilon_{\max}$ in $\mathrm{M^{-1}cm^{-1}}$ — how allowed the transition is. Spans six orders of magnitude across this module.

Width

Bands are hundreds of $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ wide because the excited state has a different equilibrium geometry. Sharp lines are the exception, and they are diagnostic.

$$ A = \varepsilon\,c\,l, \qquad \tilde\nu\,[\mathrm{cm^{-1}}] = \frac{10^{7}}{\lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}]} $$

Wavenumber, not wavelength, is the working unit throughout, for one reason: it is linear in energy. Two bands 8000 $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ apart are separated by the same energy wherever they sit, whereas a 50 nm gap means something different in the blue than in the red. Every diagram in this module has a $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ axis.

Colour is the complement of the absorption, not the absorption. $[\mathrm{Ti(H_2O)_6}]^{3+}$ absorbs at $20{,}300\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (about 493 nm, blue–green) and looks purple. Reasoning backwards from "it is purple, so it absorbs purple" inverts every assignment that follows.

2. Microstates — the Honest Starting Point

A configuration is not a state. Writing $d^2$ fixes how many electrons occupy the five $d$ orbitals but says nothing about which $(m_\ell, m_s)$ each one takes. Every distinct allowed choice is a microstate — and, from Module 04, a microstate is a Slater determinant. Counting them is a combinatorial problem, and the Pauli principle does the whole job:

$$ N_{\text{microstates}} = \binom{2(2\ell+1)}{n} = \binom{10}{n} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; d^2:\ \binom{10}{2} = 45 $$
$d^{\,n}$$d^1$$d^2$$d^3$$d^4$$d^5$$d^6$$d^7$$d^8$$d^9$

The symmetry of that row is not a coincidence — it is the first appearance of hole–electron equivalence (§6). The 252 microstates of $d^5$ are also the reason $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ have the most complicated spectra in the block.

Do not confuse a microstate with a state. The 45 microstates of $d^2$ are not 45 energy levels. Electron repulsion groups them into a handful of degenerate sets — the terms — and only those have energies. Sorting 45 determinants into terms is the whole content of §3.

Terms of $d^2$ are not the terms of two independent $d$ electrons. Naive coupling of $\ell_1 = \ell_2 = 2$ would allow $L = 0 \dots 4$ with $S = 0$ and $1$ freely — ten terms. The Pauli principle kills half of them, leaving five. Which five survive is decided by the table, not by a rule worth memorising.

4. Russell–Saunders Coupling

$$ \mathbf{L} = \sum_i \boldsymbol{\ell}_i, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \sum_i \mathbf{s}_i, \qquad \mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}, \qquad \text{term} = {}^{2S+1}L_J $$

Coupling the orbital momenta to each other first, and the spins to each other first, is an approximation with a stated condition: that electron repulsion is much larger than the spin–orbit interaction. For the $3d$ series that holds — repulsion runs at $10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, spin–orbit coupling at a few hundred. For $5d$ metals and the actinides it fails, and Module 10 replaces this scheme with $jj$ coupling. The name of the approximation is worth as much as the formula.

Symbol$S$$P$$D$$F$$G$$H$

Upper case for the many-electron term; the lower-case $s, p, d, f$ of Module 04 belong to one electron. The clash of $S$ for both a term and the spin is historical and permanent — read it from position.

Reading $^3F_2$

$2S+1 = 3 \Rightarrow S = 1$ (two unpaired electrons) · $F \Rightarrow L = 3$ · $J = 2$. Degeneracy $(2L+1)(2S+1) = 21$, split by $J$ into $J = 2, 3, 4$ only once spin–orbit coupling is switched on. In this module $J$ is carried along but never used.

1925. Henry Norris Russell was an astronomer and Frederick Saunders a spectroscopist; the scheme came out of the pressure to classify stellar absorption lines, three years before Dirac's equation and long before anyone cared about coordination complexes. Chemistry inherited a working vocabulary from astronomy, which is why the notation looks unlike anything else in inorganic chemistry.

5. Hund's Rules — and Their Range

First

Maximum $S$. Parallel spins keep electrons apart, and the exchange stabilisation of Module 04 is the reason — not any magnetic force between spins.

Second

Then maximum $L$. Electrons orbiting the same way meet less often, so repulsion is lower.

Third

Then $J$: smallest for a less-than-half-filled shell, largest beyond. This one is spin–orbit in origin, hence irrelevant until Module 10.

For $d^2$: the highest multiplicity available is a triplet, and of $^3F$ and $^3P$ the larger $L$ wins. Ground term $^3F$, and with $J$ minimal, $^3F_2$. The same procedure across the row gives the free-ion ground terms that every later diagram starts from.

Hund's rules order the ground term only. They are silent about excited terms, and used on them they give wrong answers — in $d^2$ they would place $^3P$ second, whereas the true free-ion order is $^3F < {}^1D < {}^3P < {}^1G < {}^1S$. A singlet sits below a triplet there. Excited-term energies come from the Racah parameters of §6, never from Hund.

6. Racah Parameters — Where $15B$ Comes From

Term energies are differences in electron–electron repulsion, so they are integrals over the $d$ radial function. Rather than carry those integrals, Racah repackaged them into three parameters with the property that term separations depend only on $B$ and $C$, never on $A$:

$$ E(^3F) = A - 8B, \qquad E(^3P) = A + 7B, \qquad E(^1D) = A - 3B + 2C, \qquad E(^1G) = A + 4B + 2C $$ $$ \Rightarrow\quad E(^3P) - E(^3F) = 15B $$
$A$

The total repulsion of the configuration. Identical for every term of a given $d^{\,n}$, so it cancels in every observable and is never quoted.

$B$

Separates terms of the same multiplicity. Free-ion values $700\text{--}1100\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. This is the parameter a spectrum can deliver.

$C$

Needed only when multiplicity changes. Roughly $C \approx 4B$, and that estimate is why spin-forbidden states are drawn but rarely fitted.

Racah never worked on coordination compounds. That $B$ and $C$ turned out to be the natural axes for reading the colour of a chromium salt is the second inheritance in this module from a field outside chemistry.

7. Free Ion into the Ligand Field

Switch on an octahedral field. A free-ion term is $(2L+1)$-fold degenerate in its orbital part, and $O_h$ has no irreducible representation of dimension greater than three — so any term with $L \ge 2$ must split. Which pieces appear is fixed by group theory alone, exactly as in Module 02: reduce the representation the $(2L+1)$ functions carry.

$$ ^3F \;\longrightarrow\; {}^3T_{1g} + {}^3T_{2g} + {}^3A_{2g} \qquad (3 + 3 + 1 = 7\ \checkmark) $$

Two things survive the splitting untouched. Multiplicity: the field is electrostatic and does not act on spin, so a triplet stays a triplet — the superscript is carried straight through. Barycentre: the degeneracy-weighted mean of the pieces returns the parent term energy, which is the same rule that fixed $-0.4/{+0.6}\,\Delta_o$ in Module 07.

A $P$ term never splits, and that is not a small remark. $T_{1g}$ is three-dimensional, so $^3P \to {}^3T_{1g}$ intact. In the $d^2$ and $d^3$ diagrams the $P$-derived $T_{1g}$ is therefore free to run into the $F$-derived $T_{1g}$ of the same symmetry — and two states of identical symmetry mix. The curvature of every Tanabe–Sugano diagram comes from that one off-diagonal element (§12).

End of session 1 — the states exist. Session 2 asks which transitions between them are visible.

8. Hole–Electron Equivalence

The 45 microstates of $d^2$ and of $d^8$ in §2 were the same number because two electrons in an empty $d$ shell and two holes in a full one are the same combinatorial object. The energies follow too, with one sign change: a hole has the opposite charge, so the ligand field acts on it in reverse.

$$ d^{\,n}\ \text{in}\ O_h \;\;\equiv\;\; d^{\,10-n}\ \text{in}\ O_h\ \text{with}\ \Delta_o \to -\Delta_o \;\;\equiv\;\; d^{\,10-n}\ \text{in}\ T_d $$
Consequence 1

$d^2$ and $d^7$ share one set of curves; $d^3$ and $d^8$ share another. Four of the classic three-band spectra reduce to two pieces of algebra — which is exactly what the applet in §14 exploits.

Consequence 2

The Orgel diagram of §11 can put $d^1/d^6$ on one side of a single origin and $d^4/d^9$ on the other, because the second pair is the first read backwards.

So $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$ ($d^8$, ground $^3A_{2g}$) has the term structure of $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ ($d^3$, ground $^4A_{2g}$) with the multiplicities relabelled, and $\mathrm{Co^{2+}}$ ($d^7$, ground $^4T_{1g}$) mirrors $\mathrm{V^{3+}}$ ($d^2$, ground $^3T_{1g}$).

The equivalence is between $O_h$ and $T_d$, not within $O_h$. "$d^8$ octahedral is just $d^2$ octahedral" is false — the field sign flips, which is why $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$ has an $A_{2g}$ ground state and $\mathrm{V^{3+}}$ a $T_{1g}$ one. Getting this backwards makes the first band of a nickel spectrum come out as $\Delta_o$ when it is not, or the reverse.

9. Selection Rule I — Spin

$$ \Delta S = 0 $$

The electric dipole operator $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu} = -e\sum_i \mathbf{r}_i$ contains no spin coordinate. It therefore cannot connect states of different $S$: the spin overlap factorises out and vanishes. This is not a rule of thumb; within the non-relativistic Hamiltonian of this module it is exact.

Why $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ salts are almost white

High-spin $d^5$ has ground term $^6A_{1g}$, and no other sextet exists in the $d^5$ manifold. Every $d$–$d$ transition is spin-forbidden, giving $\varepsilon \approx 0.01\text{--}0.1$. The pale pink of $\mathrm{[Mn(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}$ is the leak, not the signal.

Where the rule bends

Spin–orbit coupling mixes a little of one multiplicity into another, so "forbidden" means weak, not absent. The mixing scales steeply with $Z$ — a fact this module cannot explain and Module 10 must.

Spin-forbidden bands are recognisable on sight: they are sharp. A transition that does not change the orbital occupancy hardly changes the equilibrium bond lengths, so there is little vibrational broadening. The ruby $R$-lines at $14{,}400\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ ($^4A_{2g} \to {}^2E_g$, $\Delta S \ne 0$) are a few $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ wide, next to spin-allowed bands 2000 $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ across. Sharpness in a $d$–$d$ spectrum is a diagnosis.

10. Selection Rule II — Laporte, and How Colour Escapes It

$$ \langle \psi_f | \hat{\boldsymbol\mu} | \psi_i \rangle \ne 0 \;\;\text{requires}\;\; g \leftrightarrow u \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad g \nleftrightarrow g $$

Every $d$ orbital is even and $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}$ is odd, so in a centrosymmetric complex the $d$–$d$ integrand is odd overall and vanishes: formally octahedral complexes should be colourless. They are not, and the escape routes are this section.

Vibronic

An odd vibration momentarily destroys the inversion centre. The transition borrows intensity from that instant. This is why octahedral $d$–$d$ bands exist at all, and why they gain intensity as temperature rises.

No centre

$T_d$ has no $i$, so the rule never applies. Tetrahedral $\mathrm{[CoCl_4]^{2-}}$ runs about 100× the intensity of octahedral $\mathrm{[Co(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}$ — the deep blue against the pale pink.

$p$–$d$ mixing

Where symmetry permits odd character to mix into the $d$ set, the transition acquires allowed parentage. Formally the same escape as vibronic coupling, made permanent by geometry.

11. Orgel Diagrams — the Qualitative Picture

An Orgel diagram plots term energies against field strength, keeping only terms of the ground-state multiplicity. That restriction is what makes it readable by hand: no $C$, no spin-forbidden states, no crossings. The $D$-term case fits on one axis running both ways, because hole–electron equivalence (§8) makes the left half the right half reversed.

One term, one splitting, one band — and its position is $\Delta$ directly. This is the entire spectrum of $\mathrm{[Ti(H_2O)_6]^{3+}}$: a single absorption at $20{,}300\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, from which $\Delta_o$ is read off with no analysis at all. The $d^1$ case is the only one this easy.

What an Orgel diagram cannot do. It has no spin-forbidden states, so the ruby lines are invisible on it; it has no vertical scale, so nothing can be fitted; and it stops before the strong-field regime, so it cannot show a spin crossover. For $d^4$–$d^7$ those omissions are fatal — hence §12.

12. Tanabe–Sugano Diagrams

Tanabe and Sugano made the diagram quantitative with two decisions. First, divide both axes by $B$ — then one diagram per $d^{\,n}$ serves every ion and every ligand, because $\Delta_o/B$ and $E/B$ are dimensionless. Second, put the ground state along the horizontal axis, so every curve reads directly as a transition energy.

Axes

$x = \Delta_o/B$, $y = E/B$. A measured band at $\tilde\nu$ contributes the ratio $\tilde\nu/B$ — which is why $\Delta_o$ and $B$ come out together or not at all.

Curvature

Straight where a state is alone in its symmetry; bent where two states of the same symmetry mix. The two $^4T_{1g}$ curves below bend away from each other for exactly the reason given in §7.

Sharp lines

A nearly horizontal curve means a transition energy almost independent of the ligand — and, being nearly parallel to the ground state, one with almost no geometry change. Those are the sharp bands of §9.

$d^3$ quartets, computed from the $^4T_{1g}$ mixing matrix used by the applet in §14. The $^2E_g$ line is schematic — drawing it properly requires $C$, which no two-band spectrum can supply.

13. The Vertical Line in the $d^4$–$d^7$ Diagrams

The $d^4$ to $d^7$ diagrams carry a vertical discontinuity partway along the $x$ axis. It is neither a drawing artefact nor an avoided crossing: it is where the ground state itself changes, a low-spin term having fallen below the high-spin one.

$$ \Delta_o \lessgtr P \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{high spin} \;\big/\; \text{low spin} $$

Why the axis has to break

The convention is that the ground state lies on the horizontal axis. When the ground state swaps, the reference swaps with it, and every curve is re-zeroed. Two different diagrams are being shown side by side on one pair of axes.

Which $d^{\,n}$ break

Exactly $d^4$ to $d^7$ — the same four that had a spin choice in Module 07, for the same reason. $d^1$–$d^3$ and $d^8$–$d^9$ have no alternative configuration, so their diagrams are continuous.

The crossover condition is still Module 07's, $\Delta_o$ against the pairing energy $P$. What the diagram adds is that it reorganises the whole spectrum and not just the magnetic moment: band count and band positions change discontinuously at that line. $\mathrm{[Fe(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}}$ sit on opposite sides of it.

The applet in §14 covers only $d^2$, $d^3$, $d^7$ and $d^8$. Those four have no crossover, so one continuous set of curves is honest for them. Fitting $d^4$–$d^6$ needs to know in advance which side of the line the complex is on — a decision, not a calculation.

15. Worked Example — $\mathrm{[Cr(H_2O)_6]^{3+}}$

Step 4 is the whole procedure in one line: one measured band fixes $\Delta_o$, the second then determines $B$ through the off-diagonal element, and the third is a prediction that can fail. Here it comes out 2% high — close enough to accept the assignment, and far enough off to show where the model is thin (the $^4T_{1g}(P)$ energy is the one most sensitive to the neglected $C$).

Two bands are the minimum, and the assignment must come first. With only $\tilde\nu_1$ you have $\Delta_o$ and nothing else. With two unassigned bands you have two equations and no way to know which curve each belongs to — and swapping them yields a $B$ that is nonsense (negative, or larger than the free-ion value). A $\beta > 1$ is the signal that the assignment, not the arithmetic, is wrong.

16. The Nephelauxetic Effect — $B$ as a Measure of Covalency

$$ \beta = \frac{B_{\text{complex}}}{B_{\text{free ion}}} \;<\; 1 \quad \text{always} $$

$B$ measures electron–electron repulsion within the $d$ shell, so it falls when the $d$ electrons have more room. Covalent delocalisation onto the ligands gives exactly that: the cloud expands, repulsion drops, $B$ drops. Nephelauxetic is Jørgensen's coinage from the Greek for "cloud-expanding", and the effect is the only routine experimental measure of covalency in a complex.

Nephelauxetic series — increasing cloud expansion →
$\mathrm{F^-} < \mathrm{H_2O} < \mathrm{NH_3} < \mathrm{ox^{2-}} < \mathrm{NCS^-} < \mathrm{Cl^-} < \mathrm{CN^-} < \mathrm{Br^-} < \mathrm{I^-}$
$\beta$ typical$\approx 0.9$   →   $\approx 0.4$
This is not the spectrochemical series, and the difference is the point. The spectrochemical series of Module 08 orders ligands by $\Delta_o$ — how hard they split the $d$ set. The nephelauxetic series orders them by $\beta$ — how far the $d$ electrons spread onto them. The orders disagree: $\mathrm{I^-}$ is a weak-field ligand near the bottom of the spectrochemical series and the strongest cloud-expander of all. One number is about the field, the other about covalency, and a complex reports both from the same spectrum.

This closes the argument opened in Module 08 §10. There, covalency was inferred from where $\Delta_o$ failed to follow charge; here it is measured, in $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, from a band position. The purely ionic crystal field model of Module 07 predicts $\beta = 1$ for every complex ever made. No complex obeys it.

17. How This Theory Arrived, and Where It Stops

1954, Tokyo. Tanabe and Sugano published the diagrams in the Journal of the Physical Society of Japan, computed by hand from Racah's matrices. Their target was the absorption spectra of the ruby and of $\mathrm{MnF_2}$ — solid-state physics, not coordination chemistry; Orgel's qualitative diagrams appeared in the same years for the same reason, that the field had spectra it could not read. Within a decade they were in every inorganic textbook, and the ruby became the first laser.
What it delivers

Band positions, to a few percent, from two parameters with physical meaning. Assignments that can be tested against a third band. And $\beta$, a measurable index of covalency.

What it does not

Band intensities — those need the vibronic mechanism of §10, which the diagram knows nothing about. Band widths. And any state where $S$ has stopped being a good quantum number.

Heavy metals

For $4d$ and $5d$ ions spin–orbit coupling is not negligible, and terms stop being labelled by $S$ and $L$. Module 10.

Low symmetry

Every diagram assumes strict $O_h$ or $T_d$. A distorted or mixed-ligand complex splits bands further, and there is no axis for it.

$C$ ignored

$C$ cannot be extracted from spin-allowed bands, so the ruby $R$-lines are outside what this module computes.

The model is a fit, not a derivation. $\Delta_o$ and $B$ are read out of the spectrum they then explain. The circularity is productive — two numbers reproducing three bands is a real constraint — but it is not a first-principles calculation.

18. Module 9 Key Competencies

Count

Count the microstates of any $d^{\,n}$ and resolve $d^2$ into its five terms, closing the degeneracy sum.

Write

Give the free-ion ground term of any $d^{\,n}$, and the condition for Russell–Saunders coupling to hold.

Split

Reduce a free-ion term in $O_h$ and say why multiplicity and barycentre survive the field.

Explain

Account for an $\varepsilon$ from 0.01 to 10⁵ with both rules and one escape route, and reject an assignment on intensity grounds.

Extract

Get $\Delta_o$ and $B$ from two assigned bands of a $d^3$ or $d^8$ complex, predict the third, compute $\beta$.

Criticise

Explain the vertical discontinuity in the $d^4$–$d^7$ diagrams, and name one thing this module cannot address.

Self-test. One sentence each — (i) why $\mathrm{[Mn(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}$ is nearly colourless while $\mathrm{MnO_4^-}$ is intensely purple; (ii) why the second band of a $d^3$ spectrum fixes $B$ but the first cannot; (iii) why $\mathrm{I^-}$ is low in the spectrochemical series and top of the nephelauxetic one.
