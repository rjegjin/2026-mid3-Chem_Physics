Lecture 10B: The Sixth Row Is Not an Extrapolation of the Fifth

0. Scope

Central question

Every bonding model so far has been non-relativistic. Where does that assumption break, and what does it cost?

Modules 05 to 09 built molecular orbitals, ligand fields and electronic states on the Schrödinger equation, and applied them to $3d$, $4d$ and $5d$ metals with the same rules. That assumes the $5d$ row is the $3d$ row with bigger orbitals. It is not: somewhere between silver and gold, core electrons move fast enough that their mass is no longer the rest mass, and the valence shell they screen is rearranged as a result.

Requires

Module 04 — hydrogenic orbitals and screening · Modules 07–08 — $\Delta_o$ and the spectrochemical series · Module 10 — spin–orbit coupling, which is itself one of the three effects catalogued here

Feeds

Module 11 — why $5d$ organometallics behave unlike their $4d$ analogues · Module 13 — gold and platinum catalysis · Module 15 — heavy-element materials

This module is about bonding, not about atoms. The atomic side — $LS$ versus $jj$ coupling, magnetic moments, the Zeeman effect — belongs to Module 10 and is assumed. What is new is that a correction to an orbital propagates into a bond length, a bond energy, a $\Delta_o$, and finally a catalytic cycle.

1. One Dimensionless Number

There is no need for a heavy formalism to see that something must go wrong. Take the Bohr model of a hydrogenic $1s$ electron and ask how fast it moves.

$$ \frac{v_{1s}}{c} = Z\alpha, \qquad \alpha = \frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}\hbar c} \approx \frac{1}{137.036} $$

That is the entire input. For hydrogen $v/c = 0.7\%$ and nothing happens. For gold, $Z = 79$, it is 58 per cent of the speed of light, and the electron's mass is

$$ m_{\mathrm{rel}} = \gamma\, m_{e}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - (Z\alpha)^{2}}} = 1.22 \ \ (\mathrm{Au}) $$

The Bohr radius carries $1/m$. A mass twenty-two per cent larger is an orbital eighteen per cent smaller, from arithmetic alone. Nothing in this slide is an approximation to a hard calculation; it is a two-line estimate that happens to land within a few per cent of the Dirac–Fock answer, which is why it has survived in textbooks since Pyykkö's 1988 review.

Where the argument is crude. It is the $1s$ shell that moves at $0.58c$, not the $6s$ valence shell that does the chemistry. The connection between the two is §3 and §4, and it is the only genuinely non-obvious step in this module.

2. Three Effects, One Equation

Doing it properly means replacing the Schrödinger Hamiltonian with Dirac's, whose solutions are four-component spinors. Expanding the positive-energy branch in powers of $(v/c)^2$ gives the Pauli Hamiltonian, and the leading corrections separate into exactly three terms that chemists name separately.

$$ \hat H \approx \hat H_{\mathrm{Schr}} \;\underbrace{-\;\frac{\hat p^{4}}{8m^{3}c^{2}}}_{\text{mass–velocity}} \;+\;\underbrace{\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8m^{2}c^{2}}\nabla^{2}V}_{\text{Darwin}} \;+\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{2m^{2}c^{2}}\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\hat{\mathbf L}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf S}}_{\text{spin–orbit}} $$
Direct · scalar

Mass–velocity and Darwin together contract and stabilise shells with density at the nucleus — $s$, and $p_{1/2}$. §3.

Indirect · scalar

The contracted $s$ shells screen better, so $d$ and $f$ feel a smaller effective charge and expand and destabilise. §4.

Spin–orbit

Splits every $l > 0$ shell into $j = l \pm \tfrac12$. Not a correction to a number but a change of quantum labels. §5.

The first two are called scalar relativistic effects and can be folded into an ordinary one-component calculation. The third cannot, and it is the reason heavy-element quantum chemistry is qualitatively harder than light-element quantum chemistry.

1929. Dirac wrote that the relativistic corrections were "of no importance in the consideration of atomic and molecular structure and ordinary chemical reactions". He was wrong, and it took until the 1970s — Desclaux's numerical Dirac–Fock atoms, Pitzer's and Pyykkö's analyses of gold — for the field to say so out loud.

3. The Direct Effect — Contraction

Mass–velocity and Darwin both scale with the electron density at the nucleus, $|\psi(0)|^{2}$, which is non-zero only for $s$ orbitals and, once spin–orbit is included, for $p_{1/2}$. Those shells contract; every other shell is untouched at this stage.

Why the valence $6s$ and not just the $1s$

All $ns$ orbitals of the same atom must stay mutually orthogonal. When the $1s$ shrinks, every higher $s$ shell has to reshape its inner lobes to remain orthogonal to it, and it contracts too — by a comparable fraction, not a comparable distance. This is the step that carries a core effect out to the valence shell where chemistry happens.

What contracts, in order

Contraction and stabilisation are two names for one thing: a smaller orbital sits deeper in the nuclear potential, so the $6s$ ionisation energy rises. Gold's first ionisation energy is 9.23 eV against silver's 7.58 eV, and gold's electron affinity is 2.31 eV — close to iodine's 3.06 eV, and the reason the $\mathrm{Au^-}$ ion is a real chemical species in $\mathrm{CsAu}$.

CsAu is not a metal. It is a transparent yellow-green semiconductor with the CsCl structure, an ionic caesium auride. An alloy of two metals has no business being a semiconductor; a stabilised $6s$ that can hold an extra electron does.

4. The Indirect Effect — $d$ and $f$ Expand

The $d$ and $f$ shells have a node at the nucleus, so the direct terms do not touch them. They still move, for a reason that is pure electrostatics rather than relativity: the shells screening them have changed.

$$ s\ \text{contracts} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{screening of }d,f\ \text{improves} \;\Longrightarrow\; Z_{\mathrm{eff}}(d,f)\ \text{falls} \;\Longrightarrow\; \langle r\rangle_{d,f}\ \text{grows} $$

So the same relativistic correction pushes the two valence shells of a heavy transition metal in opposite directions. The $6s$ comes down and in; the $5d$ goes up and out. The gap between them narrows from both ends at once, and that single sentence is the engine of §8 through §14.

Consequence 1

The $5d \to 6s$ excitation energy collapses. In gold it lands in the visible. §8.

Consequence 2

An expanded, destabilised $5d$ is a better donor and overlaps ligands further out. Bigger $\Delta_o$. §12.

Consequence 3

A contracted $6s$ is harder to ionise and harder to hybridise. Mercury and the inert pair. §9, §10.

A useful check on the story. If the $d$ expansion were a direct relativistic effect, it would grow smoothly with $Z$ like $\gamma$ does. It does not: it tracks the $s$ contraction of the same atom, which is why the whole pattern peaks at gold rather than at radon. §6.

5. Spin–Orbit Coupling as a Bonding Effect

Module 10 treated $\hat H_{SO} = \lambda\,\hat{\mathbf L}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf S}$ as a perturbation on atomic states. Here the question is different: when is it large enough to change a bond? The one-electron constant scales steeply,

$$ \zeta_{nl} \;\propto\; \frac{Z_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4}}{n^{3}\,l\,(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)} $$

so a fourth-power dependence turns a footnote in the first transition series into a leading term in the sixth row.

Two consequences that later modules cash in. First, in the $5d$ row spin is no longer a good quantum number, so the spin selection rule of Module 09 fails: iridium(III) complexes phosphoresce efficiently from formally triplet states, which is exactly why they are the emitters in OLED displays. Second, in the heavy $p$ block the $6p_{1/2}$–$6p_{3/2}$ splitting is comparable to the promotion energies that hybridisation needs, so the notion of an $sp^3$ hybrid quietly stops meaning anything.

Where this module and Module 10 divide. There, spin–orbit coupling splits states of a free ion. Here, it decides whether a molecule has a bond. $\mathrm{Tl_2}$ and $\mathrm{Pb_2}$ are bound almost entirely by $p_{3/2}$ character, and a non-relativistic calculation gets their bond orders wrong.

Why gold and not radon. Two things fight. $\gamma$ grows with $Z$, but the $6s$ shell is also being progressively pulled into the core as the $5d$ fills. At gold the $5d$ shell is complete and the $6s$ holds a single electron with nothing shielding it; one step further, mercury's $6s^2$ closes and the ratio rebounds. The maximum is where the $6s^1$ configuration and the largest $\gamma$ of the row coincide.

7. Not the Lanthanide Contraction

The sixth row has a second, entirely non-relativistic reason to be small: fourteen $4f$ electrons screen badly, so $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ jumps across the lanthanides and every element after them is contracted. Zirconium and hafnium have almost identical radii for that reason, and their separation is notoriously difficult. The question is how much of the sixth row's anomaly is that, and how much is relativity.

How the two are separated

By running the same calculation twice: once with the Dirac Hamiltonian and once with $c \to \infty$. The difference is relativity by construction. Nobody can do that experiment in a laboratory, which is why this particular question was settled by computation and not by measurement.

The answer

For the Zr/Hf pair, roughly 15–20 per cent of the contraction is relativistic and the rest is $4f$ screening. For gold's $6s$, essentially all of the anomaly is relativistic: the non-relativistic gold atom would have a $6s$ shell close in size to silver's, and would be silver-coloured.

$$ \text{observed anomaly} \;=\; \underbrace{4f\ \text{screening}}_{\text{scales with the number of }f\text{ electrons}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{relativity}}_{\text{scales as }(Z\alpha)^{2}} $$

The two happen to switch on at the same place in the periodic table, which is why they were conflated for fifty years. They can still be told apart by a signature: the $4f$ contraction affects all shells alike, whereas relativity contracts $s$ and expands $d$. Any property that depends on the $6s$–$5d$ gap rather than on overall size is therefore diagnostic of relativity alone — and the colour of gold is exactly such a property.

8. Case — Why Gold Is Yellow

A metal's colour is set by its interband absorption edge: the lowest energy at which a filled band can be excited into the conduction band. In the coinage metals that is a $(n-1)d \to ns$ transition.

MetalTransitionEdgeAbsorbsReflects
Ag$4d \to 5s$$\approx 3.7\ \mathrm{eV}$ultravioletthe whole visible range — white
Au$5d \to 6s$$\approx 2.4\ \mathrm{eV}$blue, from about 520 nm downgreen through red — yellow

The gap in gold is smaller by 1.3 eV, and §3 and §4 already said why: the $6s$ has been stabilised and the $5d$ destabilised, so the two ends of the transition have moved toward each other. Remove relativity from the calculation and gold's edge climbs back into the ultraviolet, where it would look like silver.

The same argument, one element over

Caesium is the other visibly golden metal, and for a completely different reason — a low plasma frequency, not a $d \to s$ edge. Two yellow metals, two mechanisms. Naming the mechanism is the whole skill here.

And why copper is not the answer either

Copper is reddish with a $\approx 2.1\ \mathrm{eV}$ edge, and relativity is negligible for $Z = 29$. Its small $3d \to 4s$ gap comes from the poor radial extension of $3d$, not from $\gamma$. Same colour mechanism, non-relativistic cause.

1976. Christensen and Seraphin's band-structure calculation reproduced the yellow of gold only when the relativistic terms were kept, and Pitzer and Pyykkö made the general case for chemistry over the following decade. The observation is prehistoric; the explanation is younger than the pocket calculator.

9. Case — Why Mercury Is a Liquid

Mercury melts at $-38.8\ ^\circ$C. Cadmium, directly above it, melts at $321\ ^\circ$C, and gold, directly beside it, at $1064\ ^\circ$C. Nothing about size or mass explains a drop of that scale.

The argument

Mercury is $[\mathrm{Xe}]4f^{14}5d^{10}6s^{2}$. The relativistically contracted, stabilised $6s^{2}$ pair is held tightly and is reluctant to delocalise into a metallic band. Metallic bonding in mercury is therefore weak and closer to van der Waals than to the $s$–$d$ bonding that holds gold together — and a weakly bound solid melts near room temperature.

The evidence beyond hand-waving

A 2013 Angewandte Chemie study by Calvo, Pahl, Wormit and Schwerdtfeger ran molecular dynamics on mercury with and without relativistic terms. The relativistic melting point came out near the experimental value; switching relativity off raised it by roughly 105 K. That is a computed number attached to a specific claim, which is what separates this from a story.

The same closed, contracted $6s^{2}$ explains the rest of mercury's oddities as a set rather than one at a time: the highest first ionisation energy of any metal (10.44 eV), a boiling point 300 degrees below cadmium's, the existence of $\mathrm{Hg_2^{2+}}$ with a genuine Hg–Hg bond where zinc and cadmium analogues barely survive, and mercury's willingness to behave as a pseudo-noble gas in the gas phase.

The honest caveat. $\mathrm{HgF_4}$ was reported in a matrix in 2007, which would make mercury a transition metal after all by using its $5d$. It has not been reproduced in bulk. Treat "mercury is a post-transition metal" as a strong generalisation with one contested exception, not as a law.

10. Case — The Inert-Pair Effect, Properly Stated

Down groups 13 to 15 the oxidation state two below the group maximum becomes the stable one: $\mathrm{Tl^{+}}$ over $\mathrm{Tl^{3+}}$, $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ over $\mathrm{Pb^{4+}}$, $\mathrm{Bi^{3+}}$ over $\mathrm{Bi^{5+}}$. The traditional name says the $6s^{2}$ pair is "inert". That is a label, not a mechanism, and taken literally it is wrong.

Half the answer — relativistic

The $6s$ is contracted and stabilised, so promoting it to a bonding hybrid costs more than it does in the fifth row. Spin–orbit splitting of the $6p$ adds to the cost, because the $6p_{1/2}$ that would take part is itself pulled down and away from $6p_{3/2}$.

The other half — not relativistic

Bond energies fall down the group anyway, because the valence orbitals are diffuse and overlap poorly. Two extra bonds must repay the promotion energy, and heavy-element bonds are too weak to do it. Both terms are needed; either alone gets the trend but not the magnitudes.

$$ \mathrm{E^{n+}} \to \mathrm{E^{(n+2)+}}: \quad \Delta E = \underbrace{E_{\mathrm{promotion}}(6s^{2} \to 6s^{1}6p^{1})}_{\text{raised by relativity}} \;-\; \underbrace{2\,D(\mathrm{E\!-\!X})}_{\text{falls down the group}} $$

Stated this way the effect predicts instead of restating. With a strongly bonding partner the pair is not inert at all: $\mathrm{PbF_4}$ and $\mathrm{BiF_5}$ exist, because fluorine repays the promotion. $\mathrm{PbI_4}$ does not.

A second consequence. A literally inert pair would be spherical and structurally invisible. Instead $\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Bi^{3+}}$ show stereochemically active lone pairs that distort their polyhedra. The pair is reluctant to bond, not absent.

11. Bond Lengths and Bond Energies

A contracted valence $s$ shell contracts the bonds it forms. The effect is largest for bonds with the most $6s$ character, so hydrides and simple diatomics are the clean test cases.

The row inversion in the middle line is the point worth carrying away. Ordinarily a sixth-row bond is longer than its fifth-row analogue because the atom is bigger. Au–H breaks that rule, and every rule of thumb built on "size increases down a group" has to be checked before it is applied to gold, mercury or thallium.

Bond contraction maximum

Plotted across the sixth row, the relativistic bond contraction peaks at gold for the same reason §6 gave — it follows the $6s$ curve.

Where it vanishes

Bonds made from $5d$ rather than $6s$ character move the other way: the expanded $5d$ lengthens them slightly. Direction depends on which shell does the bonding.

12. Relativity Inside the Ligand Field

Modules 07 and 08 recorded, without explaining, that $\Delta_o$ grows by $30$–$50$ per cent from $3d$ to $4d$ and again from $4d$ to $5d$. Part of that is simply radial extension. In the sixth row a second term is added: the relativistically expanded $5d$ reaches further toward the ligands, so the antibonding $e_g^{*}$ combination is pushed higher.

Three results follow that were asserted in earlier modules and can now be derived.

Low spin, always

$\Delta_o$ exceeds the pairing energy for every $4d$ and $5d$ complex. The Tanabe–Sugano discontinuity of Module 09 is a $3d$ phenomenon and nothing else.

Square planar $\mathrm{Pt(II)}$

For $d^{8}$, a large enough $\Delta_o$ makes the tetragonal distortion to square planar unconditional. $\mathrm{Ni(II)}$ hesitates; $\mathrm{Pd(II)}$ and $\mathrm{Pt(II)}$ do not. Cisplatin's geometry is not an accident of its ligands.

Bigger nephelauxetic effect

The expanded $5d$ reduces interelectronic repulsion, so Racah $B$ falls further below its free-ion value than in the $3d$ row — the $\beta$ of Module 09 §18, given a cause.

Do not over-claim. Radial extension alone would raise $\Delta_o$ down a group even in a non-relativistic world. Relativity adds to a trend that already exists; the honest statement is that it accounts for a substantial minority of the $4d \to 5d$ step, not for the whole $3d \to 5d$ rise.

13. Aurophilicity

Two closed-shell $\mathrm{Au(I)}$ centres, $5d^{10}$ each, should repel. Instead they approach to 2.8–3.5 Å — shorter than the sum of the van der Waals radii, comparable to the Au–Au distance in the metal — with an attraction of 5–15 kcal mol$^{-1}$, the strength of a hydrogen bond. Pyykkö named it aurophilicity in 1988.

What it is

Dispersion — correlation between the two $5d^{10}$ shells — amplified by relativity twice over. The expanded, destabilised $5d$ is more polarisable, and the contracted, low-lying $6s$/$6p$ are close enough in energy to mix in, so the interaction gains partial covalent character. It disappears from any calculation that omits electron correlation, and weakens sharply from any that omits relativity.

What it does

It organises solids and solutions: gold(I) thiolate drugs oligomerise through it, $\mathrm{[Au(CN)_2]^-}$ salts change colour when the chains are compressed, and many gold(I) complexes are luminescent only when aggregated. Grinding a colourless powder into a glowing one is a lecture demonstration of an interaction that has no non-relativistic explanation.

The effect is a family, not a curiosity. Analogous metallophilic contacts run down the closed-shell heavy metals — $\mathrm{Ag(I)\cdots Ag(I)}$ weaker, $\mathrm{Hg(II)}$, $\mathrm{Tl(I)}$, $\mathrm{Pt(II)\cdots Pt(II)}$ stacking in the one-dimensional platinum chain compounds — and the strength tracks the relativistic curve of §6, peaking at gold.

A test that could have failed. Aurophilicity was predicted to survive in the gas phase and in solution, not merely to be a crystal-packing artefact. Variable-temperature NMR later found gold(I) aggregates persisting in solution, which is the observation that ruled packing out.

14. Gold and Platinum Catalysis

Gold was regarded as catalytically dead until the 1980s. It is now the standard catalyst for adding nucleophiles across alkynes, and the reason is the orbital picture of §3 and §4 rather than anything about gold's nobility.

$\mathrm{Au(I)}$ as a carbophilic $\pi$ acid

The relativistically stabilised $6s$ drags the LUMO of a linear $\mathrm{[LAu]^{+}}$ fragment down, making it a strong $\sigma$ acceptor from an alkyne $\pi$ bond. The contracted $6s$ is a poor back-donor at the same time, so the alkyne is activated and not merely bound. In Module 11's Dewar–Chatt–Duncanson language: strong donation, weak back-donation — exactly the imbalance that makes a bound alkyne electrophilic.

Why gold rarely does redox

Oxidative addition needs $\mathrm{Au(I)} \to \mathrm{Au(III)}$, which costs the stabilised $6s$ pair. Palladium pays that price routinely; gold usually will not, so gold catalysis is dominated by $\pi$ activation rather than by cross-coupling cycles. This is the inert-pair logic of §10 reappearing as a mechanistic preference.

Platinum illustrates the opposite branch. Its expanded $5d$ makes $\mathrm{Pt(0)}$ an excellent back-donor and $\mathrm{Pt(II)}$ a robust square-planar centre, which is why platinum runs hydrosilylation and hydrogenation where palladium runs coupling. A single relativistic shift — $6s$ down, $5d$ up — sorts three neighbouring metals into three different catalytic trades.

Forward reference. Module 12 treats oxidative addition and reductive elimination as elementary steps and Module 13 assembles them into cycles. Both will assume the argument of this slide: whether a metal will change oxidation state by two is a question about its valence $s$ shell, and in the sixth row that shell is relativistic.

15. $4f$ Against $5f$

Lanthanide chemistry is famously ionic and famously uniform: the $4f$ orbitals are buried inside the filled $5s$ and $5p$ shells, take almost no part in bonding, and give sharp, ligand-insensitive $f\!-\!f$ spectra. The actinides start out looking similar and then diverge, and the divergence is relativistic.

Two practical consequences. First, the linear uranyl ion $\mathrm{UO_2^{2+}}$ has no lanthanide analogue, and its linearity comes from $5f$ and $6d$ participation that a purely ionic model cannot produce. Second, and industrially: separating the trivalent actinides from the trivalent lanthanides in spent nuclear fuel exploits exactly this residual $5f$ covalency, using soft N- and S-donor extractants that bind $\mathrm{Am^{3+}}$ measurably better than $\mathrm{Eu^{3+}}$ despite near-identical radii.

The trend reverses along the row. By curium the $5f$ shell has contracted enough to behave much like $4f$, and the late actinides are lanthanide-like again. "Actinides are covalent" is true of the early actinides only.

16. Where the Periodic Table Stops Predicting

At the bottom of the table $(Z\alpha)^{2}$ is no longer a small parameter, and periodic-table reasoning — "it is under X, so it behaves like X" — starts to fail outright. These elements exist a few atoms at a time, for seconds, so almost everything here is computed and only some of it is tested.

Copernicium, $Z=112$

Under mercury. The $7s^{2}$ is contracted so hard that Cn is predicted to be volatile and nearly noble — a gas or a very weakly bound liquid at room temperature. Gas-phase adsorption experiments on gold surfaces are consistent with weak, mercury-like or weaker binding.

Flerovium, $Z=114$

Under lead, so it should be a soft metal. The $7p_{1/2}$ shell is split so far below $7p_{3/2}$ that $7s^{2}7p_{1/2}^{2}$ is effectively a closed shell. Experiments have disagreed about how noble it is; the honest position is that it is far less metallic than lead.

Oganesson, $Z=118$

Under radon. Calculations predict that spin–orbit splitting smears the electron shells into a nearly uniform, Thomas–Fermi-like distribution, and that Og is a solid with substantial electron affinity. A noble gas that is neither gaseous nor noble.

The lesson is not the trivia but that the periodic table is an approximation whose accuracy degrades with $Z$. Column analogies are strong in row 4, strained in row 6, broken in row 7.

A live question. Relativistic calculations put the end of the table near $Z \approx 172$, where the $1s$ level would dive into the negative-energy continuum — model-dependent, untestable, and quoted here as the boundary of an extrapolation rather than a prediction.

17. How a Calculation Handles This

Solving the four-component Dirac equation for a molecule is possible and expensive. Almost all practical heavy-element chemistry uses one of three approximations, and knowing which was used is part of reading a paper critically.

MethodWhat it doesCostWatch for
Relativistic ECP / pseudopotentialReplaces the core by a potential fitted to atomic Dirac results; the valence calculation stays non-relativisticlowestcore properties are unavailable by construction; results depend on the ECP's fitting set
ZORA / DKHTwo-component: transforms the Dirac Hamiltonian to a scalar part plus an explicit spin–orbit operator, all electrons keptmoderateZORA's gauge dependence; whether spin–orbit was actually switched on or only the scalar part
Four-component Dirac–CoulombThe full thing, plus correlation on tophighestreserved for benchmarks and small molecules

Two habits follow. First, "relativistic" without qualification usually means scalar relativistic, with spin–orbit omitted — adequate for gold's bond lengths, inadequate for thallium's or for anything phosphorescent. Second, relativity and electron correlation are not additive: aurophilicity is the standard counter-example, since it needs both simultaneously and vanishes if either is dropped.

What this module cannot do. It gives no quantitative predictions. Every number quoted has come from a calculation or an experiment, and the arguments here explain trends and signs rather than generate values. Anyone needing a bond length for a $5d$ complex should run a calculation with a relativistic ECP, not extrapolate from §11.

18. Competencies

By the end of this module the following should be possible without notes.

Estimate

Compute $v/c = Z\alpha$ and $\gamma$ for any element and state the resulting $1s$ contraction, then say why that number is not the valence contraction.

Distinguish

Separate the direct effect (s, p1/2 contract), the indirect effect (d, f expand) and spin–orbit splitting, and assign an observation to the right one.

Explain

Account for the colour of gold, the liquidity of mercury, the inert-pair effect and aurophilicity from the $6s$/$5d$ shift, in one causal chain each.

Criticise

Say what fraction of the sixth-row anomaly is relativistic rather than the $4f$ contraction, and name the computational experiment that separates them.

Self-test. One sentence each — (i) why the $6s$ contraction peaks at gold rather than growing to radon; (ii) why $\mathrm{PbF_4}$ exists but $\mathrm{PbI_4}$ does not; (iii) why no high-spin $5d$ complex is known; (iv) why aurophilicity disappears from a Hartree–Fock calculation even when relativity is included. If any sticks, return to §6, §10, §12, §13.
