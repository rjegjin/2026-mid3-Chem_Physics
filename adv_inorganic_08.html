Lecture 08: $\sigma$ Frameworks, $\pi$ Donors and Acceptors

0. The Other Half of the Same Picture

Module 07 ended on a failure. Crystal field theory predicts the pattern of the splitting correctly and cannot explain its size — charged $\mathrm{OH^-}$ turns out to be a weaker field than neutral $\mathrm{NH_3}$, which a point-charge model has no mechanism for.

What stays

  • ● The labels $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$, unchanged. They come from symmetry (Modules 01–02), and symmetry does not care which bonding model we use.
  • ● $\Delta_o$ as the quantity that decides spin state, colour and magnetism.
  • ● Every CFSE result of Module 07 §5.

What changes

  • ● Ligands stop being point charges and become orbitals.
  • ● $\Delta_o$ stops being an electrostatic repulsion and becomes an antibonding gap.
  • ● And then the series follows — not as a list, but as a conclusion.
Nothing new is needed to do this. The ligand SALCs were already derived in Module 06 §7, and the same $\Gamma_\sigma$ appeared there for $\mathrm{SF_6}$. This module assembles Module 06's machinery on a metal centre — that is all.

1. The $\sigma$ Framework

Take the six ligand lone pairs as a basis and reduce. This was done in Module 06; the result is recovered, not re-derived.

$$ \Gamma_\sigma \;=\; A_{1g} \;+\; E_g \;+\; T_{1u} $$

Why only these three labels

Six equivalent functions can only combine into six symmetry-adapted ones, and the reduction formula of Module 02 says how they group: one $A_{1g}$ (all in phase), one $E_g$ pair, one $T_{1u}$ set of three. $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$ — the count must close, and it does.

Note what is absent. There is no $T_{2g}$ in $\Gamma_\sigma$. Not "a little" — none at all. Six inward-pointing lone pairs cannot form a combination that transforms as $T_{2g}$.

That absence is the single most consequential fact in this module, and §2 spends its whole time on it.

Same conclusion, two routes

Module 07 said $t_{2g}$ points between the ligands and so is repelled least. Module 08 says $t_{2g}$ has no symmetry partner and so does not interact at all. These are the same geometric fact stated in two languages — and the second one is exact rather than approximate.

The orbital count

Nine metal valence orbitals ($s + 3p + 5d$) and six ligand SALCs give fifteen molecular orbitals: six bonding, three non-bonding ($t_{2g}$), six antibonding. Orbitals are conserved, exactly as in Module 05 §1.

The bonding orbitals lie low and are mostly ligand in character — they are the M–L bonds. The antibonding ones lie high and are mostly metal.

3. The $\sigma$-Only Diagram — Where $\Delta_o$ Actually Lives

Read the two levels that matter

$t_{2g}$ sits exactly where the metal $d$ orbitals were — no partner, no shift. $e_g^*$ has been pushed up, because it is the antibonding partner of the ligand $E_g$ combination.

$$ \Delta_o \;=\; E(e_g^{*}) \;-\; E(t_{2g}) $$

The immediate payoff

A stronger $\sigma$ donor overlaps better with the metal $E_g$ orbitals, pushes $e_g^*$ higher, and therefore makes $\Delta_o$ larger. Crystal field theory had no such lever — a point charge has no overlap to be good or bad at.

4. $\Delta_o$ Renamed

The same symbol, the same measured number, a different physical object.

Crystal field theoryLigand field theory
Ligand isa negative point chargea set of orbitals
$e_g$ is raised becauseit points at the chargesit is antibonding
$t_{2g}$ is lowered becauseit points between the chargesit is non-bonding — it never moved
$\Delta_o$ isan electrostatic splittinga $\sigma$-antibonding gap
Ligand order isunexplaineda consequence of overlap
Note the third row carefully. In the CFT picture both sets move — one up, one down, about a barycentre. In the $\sigma$-only LFT picture $t_{2g}$ does not move at all; the whole of $\Delta_o$ comes from raising $e_g^*$. The barycentre rule of Module 07 §2 is an artefact of the electrostatic model, not a law.

Why Module 07 still works

Every CFSE calculation used only the relative spacing of $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$, expressed in units of $\Delta_o$. Reinterpreting where $\Delta_o$ comes from leaves those numbers untouched. A wrong mechanism with the right symmetry gives the right pattern — which is what Module 07 §11 concluded.

5. Bringing In $\pi$ — and Who Is Waiting

Each ligand has orbitals perpendicular to the M–L axis as well. Six ligands, two each, gives twelve $\pi$ functions. Reducing them:

$$ \Gamma_\pi \;=\; T_{1g} \;+\; T_{2g} \;+\; T_{1u} \;+\; T_{2u} $$

$T_{2g}$ — the one that matters

This matches the metal $t_{2g}$ set, which the $\sigma$ framework left completely free. An empty dance partner has finally arrived.

$T_{1u}$ — already spoken for

Matches metal $p$, but those are deeply committed to $\sigma$ bonding. Conventionally neglected; a full treatment includes it as a small correction.

$T_{1g}$, $T_{2u}$ — nobody home

No metal valence orbital carries these labels. They stay non-bonding, unchanged, and play no part.

The whole of $\pi$ chemistry now hinges on one question: are the ligand $T_{2g}$ orbitals filled and low, or empty and high? Those two cases push $t_{2g}$ in opposite directions, and they are §6 and §7. There is no third case.

6. $\pi$ Donors — $\Delta_o$ Shrinks

A halide brings filled $p$ orbitals lying below the metal $d$ level. Filled meets non-bonding.

The mechanism, step by step

Filled ligand $\pi$ below, non-bonding metal $t_{2g}$ above. They mix into a lower bonding pair (mostly ligand) and an upper antibonding pair (mostly metal). The four electrons that were in the ligand orbitals fill the lower one; the metal $d$ electrons now sit in an antibonding $t_{2g}$.

$$ t_{2g} \uparrow \;,\quad e_g^{*} \text{ fixed} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \Delta_o \downarrow $$
Filled–filled interactions are destabilising. Module 05 §2 showed the antibonding level rises more than the bonding level falls. So $\pi$ donation from a halide does not merely shrink $\Delta_o$ — it costs the complex energy. This is why halide complexes are labile and why hard halides sit at the weak-field end.

7. $\pi$ Acceptors — $\Delta_o$ Grows

$\mathrm{CO}$ and $\mathrm{CN^-}$ bring empty $\pi^*$ orbitals lying above the metal $d$ level. Empty meets occupied — the opposite arrangement.

The mechanism

Metal $t_{2g}$ below, empty ligand $\pi^*$ above. The lower combination is now bonding and it is the one the metal $d$ electrons occupy. They are stabilised, $t_{2g}$ drops, $e_g^*$ is untouched.

$$ t_{2g} \downarrow \;,\quad e_g^{*} \text{ fixed} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \Delta_o \uparrow $$

Back-donation, and how it is measured

Electron density flows metal $\to$ ligand $\pi^*$. Filling an antibonding orbital of $\mathrm{CO}$ weakens the C–O bond, so $\nu_{\mathrm{CO}}$ falls — 2143 cm$^{-1}$ free, 2057 in $\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}$, 1790 in $\mathrm{[Fe(CO)_4]^{2-}}$. Module 05 §11 derived those frontier orbitals; here they do work.

Unlike §6, this is an empty–filled interaction: purely stabilising, with no antibonding penalty to pay.

8. The Spectrochemical Series, Derived

$$ \mathrm{I^-} < \mathrm{Br^-} < \mathrm{Cl^-} < \mathrm{F^-} < \mathrm{OH^-} < \mathrm{H_2O} < \mathrm{NH_3} < \mathrm{en} < \mathrm{NO_2^-} < \mathrm{CN^-} \approx \mathrm{CO} $$

Sort the ligands into the three classes of §5–§7 and the order is no longer a list to learn.

▼ π donor

$\mathrm{I^-}$, $\mathrm{Br^-}$, $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, $\mathrm{F^-}$, $\mathrm{OH^-}$

Filled $\pi$ raises $t_{2g}$. Weak field end.

▬ σ only

$\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{en}$, amines

Nothing of $\pi$ symmetry to offer. $t_{2g}$ stays put. Middle.

▲ π acceptor

$\mathrm{CN^-}$, $\mathrm{CO}$, $\mathrm{NO_2^-}$, phen

Empty $\pi^*$ lowers $t_{2g}$. Strong field end.

The order within the halides needs the same reasoning. $\mathrm{I^-}$ is the weakest field and $\mathrm{F^-}$ the strongest — the reverse of what charge density would suggest. Iodine's diffuse $5p$ orbitals sit close in energy to the metal $d$ level and overlap well, so iodide is the best $\pi$ donor and cuts $\Delta_o$ the most. Fluorine's $2p$ is far too low to donate effectively, so $\mathrm{F^-}$ acts as a nearly pure $\sigma$ donor.

Stated as a rule: a better $\pi$ donor makes a weaker field.

9. $\mathrm{H_2O}$ Against $\mathrm{NH_3}$ — the Deciding Case

This is the pair Module 07 could not handle, and the cleanest test of everything above.

$\mathrm{H_2O}$ — weaker field

Oxygen has two lone pairs. One forms the $\sigma$ bond to the metal; the other has $\pi$ symmetry with respect to the M–O axis and donates.

$\sigma$ donor plus $\pi$ donor ⇒ $t_{2g}$ raised ⇒ smaller $\Delta_o$.

$\mathrm{NH_3}$ — stronger field

Nitrogen has one lone pair, and it goes into the $\sigma$ bond. The other three valence orbitals are committed to N–H bonds. Nothing of $\pi$ symmetry is left.

$\sigma$ donor only ⇒ $t_{2g}$ untouched ⇒ larger $\Delta_o$.

Why this settles the argument against CFT

On charge alone, $\mathrm{OH^-}$ (anionic) should outrank $\mathrm{H_2O}$ (neutral), and both should outrank $\mathrm{NH_3}$. Observed order: $\mathrm{OH^-} < \mathrm{H_2O} < \mathrm{NH_3}$ — exactly inverted. And it follows immediately from $\pi$ donation, because $\mathrm{OH^-}$ is the better $\pi$ donor of the two oxygen ligands.

Do not over-read the series. It is an empirical average across many metals, not a physical constant. Orders can invert with the metal, its oxidation state and the geometry — and a genuine treatment of a non-octahedral complex needs the angular overlap model rather than a single $\Delta$. What is robust is the reasoning: classify by $\sigma$/$\pi$ behaviour, then predict.

10. Covalency, Measured — the Nephelauxetic Effect

If ligand orbitals really mix with metal $d$ orbitals, the $d$ electrons are no longer confined to the metal. They spread out, and spread-out electrons repel each other less.

$$ \beta \;=\; \frac{B(\text{complex})}{B(\text{free ion})} \;<\; 1 $$

What $B$ is and where it comes from

$B$ is a Racah parameter — a measure of electron–electron repulsion within the $d$ shell, read out of an absorption spectrum. Module 09 extracts it from a Tanabe–Sugano diagram alongside $\Delta_o$. The name "nephelauxetic" is Greek for cloud-expanding.

Nephelauxetic series

$\mathrm{F^-}$ < $\mathrm{H_2O}$ < $\mathrm{NH_3}$ < $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ < $\mathrm{CN^-}$ < $\mathrm{Br^-}$ < $\mathrm{I^-}$

Reading left to right, covalency increases and $\beta$ falls.

The two series are not the same order — and that is the point. Iodide is at the weak-field end of the spectrochemical series and at the most covalent end of the nephelauxetic series. There is no contradiction: $\Delta_o$ measures how far $e_g^*$ and $t_{2g}$ are pushed apart, while $\beta$ measures how far the $d$ electrons have leaked onto the ligands. Different quantities, different physics.

A ligand can therefore be a poor field and a strongly covalent partner at once. Treating the spectrochemical series as a general "strength" ranking is the mistake this slide exists to prevent.

11. Where the 18-Electron Rule Comes From

The diagram of §3 already contains it. Count the orbitals a complex can fill without paying an antibonding penalty.

LevelOrbitalsElectronsCharacter
$a_{1g}$12bonding
$t_{1u}$36bonding
$e_g$24bonding
$t_{2g}$36non-bonding
total918—

The next available orbital is $e_g^*$, which is antibonding. Filling it costs energy, so 18 is a natural stopping point.

Why $\pi$ acceptors enforce it hardest

A $\pi$ acceptor pushes $t_{2g}$ down (§7), widening the gap to $e_g^*$. The 18-electron configuration becomes a deep well. That is why carbonyls obey the rule so faithfully — $\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}$, $\mathrm{Fe(CO)_5}$, $\mathrm{Cr(CO)_6}$ are all exactly 18.

And why $\pi$ donors let it slide

A $\pi$ donor raises $t_{2g}$, shrinking the gap. Electron counts stray freely, which is exactly what the high-spin halide and aqua complexes of Module 07 do — $\mathrm{[Fe(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}$ has 20 valence electrons by this counting and is perfectly stable.

Module 11 takes this up as a counting tool. Its exceptions are not arbitrary: they track the $\pi$ character of the ligands.

12. How This Theory Arrived, and Where It Stops

Van Vleck, 1935

Having made crystal field theory useful (Module 07), he pointed out its limits himself and proposed mixing in orbital overlap. The idea existed for two decades before chemists took it up.

Orgel, Griffith, 1957

Their review named and consolidated "ligand field theory". The $\sigma$/$\pi$ classification of this module dates from that period, and it is what finally explained the series.

Since

The angular overlap model generalised it to arbitrary geometries; density functional methods now compute $\Delta_o$ rather than parameterise it. LFT survives because it explains cheaply what calculation only reproduces expensively.

What LFT still does not do. It does not predict the magnitude of $\Delta_o$ from first principles — $\Delta_o$ remains a quantity fitted to a spectrum. It is a one-electron model, so it handles electron–electron repulsion only through parameters like the Racah $B$ of §10. And a single $\Delta$ is meaningful only in high symmetry.

The honest summary: Module 07 explained the pattern, Module 08 explains the order, and neither computes the number. Getting the number is Module 09's job — and it gets it from experiment, not from theory.

The one sentence to carry forward

$\Delta_o$ is set by overlap, not by charge: $\sigma$ donation raises $e_g^{*}$, $\pi$ donation raises $t_{2g}$, $\pi$ acceptance lowers it.
