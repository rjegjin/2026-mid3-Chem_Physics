Scope note. We do not solve the many-electron Schrödinger equation. We take the hydrogenic solution as the orbital basis and then ask what electron–electron repulsion does to the ordering of that basis.

1. The Hydrogenic Schrödinger Equation

One electron, one nucleus of charge $+Ze$. The potential depends only on $r$, so the equation separates in spherical coordinates.

$$ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2 - \frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\right]\psi = E\psi \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\phi) = R_{n\ell}(r)\, Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\phi) $$

What separation buys us

  • ● The angular part $Y_\ell^m$ is universal — it does not know about $Z$ or even about the Coulomb form. Any central potential gives the same $Y_\ell^m$.
  • ● Therefore the shapes of $s, p, d, f$ orbitals survive into many-electron atoms unchanged.
  • ● Only the radial part $R_{n\ell}$ has to be re-derived when other electrons are present.

The accidental degeneracy

For the pure Coulomb potential only,

$$ E_n = -\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\cdot 13.6\ \text{eV} $$

$E$ depends on $n$ alone, so $2s$ and $2p$ are degenerate. This is a special property of $1/r$, not a general law. Break the $1/r$ form — add a second electron — and $2s \ne 2p$ immediately.

Schrödinger, 1926. The hydrogen spectrum had been fitted numerically by Bohr in 1913, but Bohr's orbits were postulated. Schrödinger's four papers of 1926 derived the same energies from a wave equation — and delivered, unasked, the quantum numbers $\ell$ and $m_\ell$ as separation constants.

2. The Four Quantum Numbers

Three arise from the boundary conditions of the differential equation. The fourth does not — it is put in by hand.

SymbolNameAllowed valuesOriginPhysical meaning
$n$Principal$1, 2, 3, \dots$Radial boundary conditionEnergy and mean radius
$\ell$Azimuthal$0, 1, \dots, n-1$Separation constantMagnitude of orbital angular momentum
$m_\ell$Magnetic$-\ell, \dots, 0, \dots, +\ell$Separation constantIts projection on a chosen axis
$m_s$Spin magnetic$+\tfrac12,\ -\tfrac12$Not from this equationProjection of intrinsic spin

Why $\ell \le n-1$

The radial equation admits a normalizable solution only if the number of radial nodes $n-\ell-1$ is a non-negative integer. A $2d$ orbital is not "rare" — it does not exist.

Degeneracy of a shell

$\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(2\ell+1) = n^2$ spatial orbitals, hence $2n^2$ spin-orbitals. This is the origin of the period lengths, once the $(n+\ell)$ ordering of §9 is applied.

Spin was an inference, not a derivation. Stern and Gerlach (1922) split a silver beam into two components — an even number, impossible for integer $\ell$. Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit (1925) proposed an intrinsic half-integer angular momentum; Pauli had already used a "two-valuedness not describable classically" to state the exclusion rule. Spin entered chemistry as a bookkeeping necessity and was only later justified by Dirac's relativistic equation (1928).

3. Radial Functions and Nodes

$$ \text{radial nodes} = n-\ell-1 $$ $$ \text{angular nodes} = \ell $$ $$ \text{total nodes} = n-1 $$

Penetration

Low-$\ell$ orbitals have radial density close to the nucleus. A $4s$ electron spends part of its time inside the $3d$ radial maximum, where it is shielded far less. This single fact drives §10 and §11.

Same $n$, different $\ell$. The inner lobes of $3s$ and $3p$ are small in area but they sit where the nuclear attraction is strongest.

4. Angular Functions and the Orbitals We Draw

The $Y_\ell^m$ with $m \ne 0$ are complex. The familiar lobed pictures are real linear combinations of them.

$$ p_x = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(Y_1^{+1} - Y_1^{-1}\right), \qquad p_y = \tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\left(Y_1^{+1} + Y_1^{-1}\right), \qquad p_z = Y_1^{0} $$

Why this matters for us

  • ● Real combinations are what transform as the rows of a character table. $\{p_x, p_y, p_z\}$ span $T_{1u}$ in $O_h$; $Y_1^{m}$ individually do not label neatly.
  • ● The five real $d$ orbitals split $2+3$ in $O_h$ precisely because $\{d_{z^2}, d_{x^2-y^2}\}$ and $\{d_{xy}, d_{xz}, d_{yz}\}$ are separately closed under the group operations. That is Module 07's entire starting point.
A real combination is not an eigenfunction of $\hat{L}_z$. $p_x$ has no definite $m_\ell$. This costs nothing while the field is cubic — but in Module 09 and 10, where $L_z$ and $J$ matter, we must go back to the complex $Y_\ell^m$ basis. Keeping the two bases straight is a common source of error.

5. Orbital Angular Momentum

$\hat{L}^2$ and $\hat{L}_z$ commute with each other and with a central-field $\hat{H}$, so all three share eigenfunctions.

$$ \hat{L}^2 Y_\ell^m = \ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2\, Y_\ell^m \qquad \hat{L}_z Y_\ell^m = m_\ell \hbar\, Y_\ell^m \qquad |\mathbf{L}| = \sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}\,\hbar $$

$\ell(\ell+1)$, not $\ell^2$

For $\ell=1$: $|\mathbf{L}| = \sqrt2\,\hbar \approx 1.41\hbar$, while $\max(m_\ell)\hbar = \hbar$. The vector is always longer than its longest projection.

So it cannot align

$\mathbf{L}$ can never lie exactly along $z$. If it did, $L_x = L_y = 0$ simultaneously, violating $[\hat{L}_x,\hat{L}_y] = i\hbar \hat{L}_z$.

Only one axis at a time

We may know $|\mathbf{L}|$ and one projection. The remaining two are indeterminate — the vector-cone picture, not an arrow.

Forward reference — Modules 09 and 10

Because $\hat{L}$ obeys these commutators, individual electron momenta add as vectors with quantised resultants: $\mathbf{L} = \sum_i \boldsymbol{\ell}_i$, $\mathbf{S} = \sum_i \mathbf{s}_i$, $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}$. Russell–Saunders coupling is nothing more than doing this sum in that order — and $jj$ coupling is doing it in the other order. Which order is correct depends on whether electron repulsion or spin–orbit coupling is the larger perturbation. That comparison is Module 10.

6. Spin Angular Momentum

Spin satisfies the same algebra as $\hat{L}$ but with a half-integer quantum number and no spatial coordinate.

$$ \hat{S}^2 \chi = s(s+1)\hbar^2 \chi = \tfrac{3}{4}\hbar^2 \chi \qquad \hat{S}_z \chi_{\pm} = \pm\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\, \chi_{\pm} \qquad s = \tfrac12 \ \text{always} $$

Not a rotating ball

A classical sphere of the electron's mass and radius spinning fast enough to give $\tfrac12\hbar$ would have a surface speed far above $c$. Spin has no classical limit; it is a label on the state, with the algebra of an angular momentum and nothing else.

Total wavefunction

A one-electron state is a spin-orbital: $\;\psi_{n\ell m}(\mathbf r)\,\chi_{m_s}$. Counting states means counting spin-orbitals, which is why $2n^2$ and not $n^2$ sets the period lengths.

The $g$-factor anomaly, previewed. The magnetic moment of spin is roughly twice what the same angular momentum in orbital form would give ($g_e \approx 2.0023$). This is why the spin-only formula $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mu_B$ of Module 07 works at all, and why orbital contributions in Module 10 must be handled separately rather than simply added.

7. The Pauli Principle Is About Antisymmetry

The rule taught first — "no two electrons share all four quantum numbers" — is a consequence, not the principle.

$$ \hat{P}_{12}\,\Psi(1,2,\dots,N) = -\,\Psi(1,2,\dots,N) $$

The many-electron wavefunction must change sign under exchange of any two electron labels. Electrons are fermions; this is a postulate about the state, not about a force.

A product violates it

$\Psi = \phi_a(1)\phi_b(2)$ is not antisymmetric. Worse, it says electron 1 is in $\phi_a$ — a statement forbidden for identical particles.

The fix

$\Psi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2) - \phi_a(2)\phi_b(1)\right]$. Swap the labels and the sign flips. Set $\phi_a = \phi_b$ and it vanishes identically.

The Fermi hole — why this has energetic consequences

Antisymmetry makes $\Psi \to 0$ as two same-spin electrons approach each other. Same-spin electrons therefore avoid one another before any Coulomb repulsion is considered. This purely statistical avoidance is the mechanism behind Hund's first rule (§9) — not "parallel spins repel less because magnets".

Pauli, 1925. Pauli arrived at exclusion by working backwards from the anomalous Zeeman effect and the observed closing of shells at 2, 8, 18. He explicitly declined to explain why. The antisymmetry formulation, and with it the connection to fermion statistics, came from Heisenberg and Dirac in 1926.

8. Slater Determinants

A determinant is antisymmetric by construction: swapping two rows changes its sign. Slater's device is to make the rows the electrons.

$$ \Psi(1,\dots,N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}} \begin{vmatrix} \phi_1(1) & \phi_2(1) & \cdots & \phi_N(1) \\ \phi_1(2) & \phi_2(2) & \cdots & \phi_N(2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \phi_1(N) & \phi_2(N) & \cdots & \phi_N(N) \end{vmatrix} $$

Rows = electrons

Exchange two electrons ⇒ exchange two rows ⇒ sign flips. Antisymmetry is automatic.

Columns = spin-orbitals

Two identical columns ⇒ determinant is zero. Pauli exclusion falls out of linear algebra.

Shorthand

$|\phi_1 \phi_2 \cdots \phi_N|$, or for He: $|1s\,\overline{1s}|$ with the bar meaning $\beta$ spin.

This is the bridge to Module 09

"How many microstates does $d^2$ have?" is exactly "how many distinct determinants can be built from the ten $3d$ spin-orbitals, taken two at a time?" — that is $\binom{10}{2} = 45$. Those 45 determinants then regroup into the terms $^3F, {}^3P, {}^1G, {}^1D, {}^1S$, whose degeneracies $21+9+9+5+1$ sum back to 45. Module 09 does that regrouping; the counting is done here.

Slater, 1929. Determinantal wavefunctions had appeared in Heisenberg's and Dirac's work, but Slater's paper made them a practical tool by showing how matrix elements of the Hamiltonian between determinants reduce to a short list of one- and two-electron integrals — the rules that still carry his name.

9. Aufbau, Hund's Rule, and Exchange Stabilisation

The $(n+\ell)$ ordering

Fill in order of increasing $n+\ell$; for equal $n+\ell$, increasing $n$. This reproduces $4s$ before $3d$ ($4+0 < 3+2$).

It is a mnemonic, not a theorem. It has no derivation from the Schrödinger equation and it fails for roughly twenty elements — Cr, Cu, Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, Pt, Au among them. Treat exceptions as data, not as misbehaviour.

Hund's first rule

For a given configuration, the term of highest multiplicity lies lowest. Then, among those, highest $L$. Then $J$: smallest for a less-than-half-filled shell, largest for more than half filled.

Why parallel spins win

Two same-spin electrons in different orbitals gain a negative exchange term $-K_{ab}$ that has no classical analogue. It exists only because of the Fermi hole of §7.

$$ E_{\uparrow\uparrow} = J_{ab} - K_{ab} \quad < \quad E_{\uparrow\downarrow} = J_{ab} $$

The stabilisation scales with the number of parallel pairs, $\binom{n_\uparrow}{2}$ — which is why the effect peaks at the half-filled shell.

$d^n$Parallel pairsExchange terms
$d^3$3$-3K$
$d^4$6$-6K$
$d^5$ high spin10$-10K$
$d^6$ high spin10 + 1$-11K$, but one pair now doubly occupies

This table is the reason $d^5$ and $f^7$ configurations are unusually stable, and it will reappear in Module 07 as the competition between $\Delta_o$ and pairing energy.

Hund, 1925. Friedrich Hund extracted his rules from atomic spectra by inspection, before any calculation could justify them. Slater and others supplied the exchange-integral explanation later in the decade. The rules are strictly reliable only for the lowest term of a configuration — using them to order excited terms is a common overreach.

10. The $ns$ / $(n-1)d$ Problem

Potassium fills $4s$, not $3d$. But in scandium the $3d$ orbital energy already lies below $4s$. Both statements are true, and the resolution is a lesson in what "orbital energy" means.

The wrong question

"Which orbital is lower?" — orbital energies are not additive. The quantity nature minimises is the total electronic energy of the whole configuration, which includes every electron–electron repulsion term.

The right comparison

Compare $E_{\text{total}}[\,3d^{n-1}4s^1\,]$ with $E_{\text{total}}[\,3d^{n}\,]$ and $E_{\text{total}}[\,3d^{n-2}4s^2\,]$. For neutral atoms of the first transition series the $4s$-containing configuration usually wins, by a small margin.

Why $4s$ is competitive at all

Penetration (§3). The $4s$ radial function has inner lobes reaching the core, where shielding is weak and $Z_{\text{eff}}$ is large.

Why $3d$ is penalised

Compactness. $3d$ is radially contracted, so two $3d$ electrons repel each other much more strongly than two diffuse $4s$ electrons do.

The margin is small

A fraction of an eV. That is why Cr is $3d^54s^1$ and Cu is $3d^{10}4s^1$: the exchange gain of §9 is enough to tip it.

And it reverses on ionisation

Remove an electron and $Z_{\text{eff}}$ rises. The compact $3d$ benefits more than the diffuse $4s$. See §11.

11. Why $s$ Electrons Leave First

Every first-row transition metal dication is $[\text{Ar}]3d^{\,n}$ — with no $4s$ electrons. Not one exception.

AtomNeutral configuration$M^{2+}$Note
Ti$[\text{Ar}]3d^2 4s^2$$[\text{Ar}]3d^2$both $4s$ removed
Cr$[\text{Ar}]3d^5 4s^1$$[\text{Ar}]3d^4$$4s$ then one $3d$
Fe$[\text{Ar}]3d^6 4s^2$$[\text{Ar}]3d^6$both $4s$ removed
Cu$[\text{Ar}]3d^{10} 4s^1$$[\text{Ar}]3d^9$$4s$ then one $3d$

The argument

In the cation, the reduced electron count means less mutual shielding, so $Z_{\text{eff}}$ rises for every orbital. The contracted $3d$ set, sitting closer in, is stabilised more steeply than the diffuse $4s$. The ordering that was marginal in the neutral atom is decisive in the ion, and $4s$ becomes the highest occupied level.

Do not say "$4s$ fills first, so $4s$ empties last". That reasoning is backwards and gives the wrong answer. Filling order is a statement about neutral-atom total energies; ionisation order is a statement about the cation's level ordering. They are different systems and there is no reason for one to determine the other.

Why Modules 07–09 depend on this slide

Crystal field theory splits a $d^n$ manifold. Ligand field theory builds MOs from metal $d$ and ligand SALCs. Both take as given that the metal centre in a complex is a $d^n$ ion with no valence $s$ electrons. That premise is established here and nowhere else in the course.
