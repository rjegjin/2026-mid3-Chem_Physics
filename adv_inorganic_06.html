Lecture 06: The Projection Operator Method and MO Diagram Construction

1. Review: LCAO-MO Theory

Molecular Orbitals ($\Psi$) are formed by the Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals ($\phi$).

$$ \Psi = c_1 \phi_1 + c_2 \phi_2 + \dots + c_n \phi_n $$

Two Primary Conditions for Interaction

  • ● Energy Proximity: Atomic orbitals must have comparable energies.
  • ● Symmetry Match: The integral $\langle \phi_i | \hat{H} | \phi_j \rangle$ is non-zero ONLY if both orbitals belong to the same IR.

2. Symmetry-Adapted Linear Combinations (SALCs)

In multi-center systems, we group equivalent atomic orbitals into **Basis Sets** that transform according to the point group of the molecule.

The SALC Goal

Instead of individual atomic orbitals, we use their linear combinations that match the IRs of the group. This decouples the secular determinant into smaller, manageable blocks.

Why SALCs?

Symmetry ensures that an orbital of $A_1$ symmetry cannot interact with an orbital of $B_1$ symmetry. SALCs automatically group orbitals by their interaction potential.

3. The Projection Operator Method

How do we mathematically derive SALCs? We use the Projection Operator ($\hat{P}$).

$$ \hat{P}^{\Gamma_i} \phi = \frac{l_i}{h} \sum_R \chi_i(R) \hat{R} \phi $$

1. Start with one basis orbital (e.g., $\phi_1$).

2. Apply all symmetry operations ($\hat{R}$) of the point group to it.

3. Multiply each result by the corresponding character ($\chi_i$) from the table.

4. Sum them up to get the SALC for representation $\Gamma_i$.

6. Central Atom Orbitals (Oxygen)

Consult the $C_{2v}$ Character Table for valence orbitals of Oxygen ($2s, 2p$):

  • $2s$ Orbital: $A_1$
  • $2p_z$ Orbital: $A_1$
  • $2p_x$ Orbital: $B_1$
  • $2p_y$ Orbital: $B_2$

Interactions possible with H SALCs ($A_1, B_2$):

  • $A_1$ (H SALC) + $2s, 2p_z$ (O)
  • $B_2$ (H SALC) + $2p_y$ (O)
  • $2p_x$ (O) remains Non-Bonding ($B_1$)

7. Constructing the MO Diagram

  1. Determine Point Group ($C_{2v}$).
  2. Find $\Gamma_{basis}$ for terminal atoms (SALCs).
  3. Decompose $\Gamma_{basis}$ into IRs.
  4. Identify symmetry of central atom orbitals.
  5. Combine orbitals of the SAME symmetry.
  6. Rank levels by node count and energy.

8. Beyond $H_2O$: $NH_3$ and $BF_3$

The procedure does not change — only the point group and the number of peripheral atoms do.

$NH_3$ — $C_{3v}$

$$ \Gamma_{\sigma}(3\times\text{H }1s) = A_1 + E $$
  • ● N $2s$ and $2p_z$ both span $A_1$ — they mix, and the surviving $A_1$ combination is the lone pair.
  • ● N $(2p_x, 2p_y)$ span $E$ — matched by the ligand $E$ set.
  • ● Result: three bonding, three antibonding, one largely non-bonding $a_1$ lone pair pointing away from the H atoms.

$BF_3$ — $D_{3h}$

$$ \Gamma_{\sigma}(3\times\text{F}) = A_1' + E' $$
  • ● B $2s$ spans $A_1'$; B $(2p_x, 2p_y)$ span $E'$. Both matched.
  • ● B $2p_z$ spans $A_2''$ — no $\sigma$ partner exists. It is left empty and perpendicular to the plane.
  • ● That vacant $a_2''$ orbital is the Lewis acidity of $BF_3$, and it accepts $\pi$ donation from the F lone pairs.

The lesson

Symmetry tells us when an orbital has no partner. An unmatched orbital is not a failure of the method — it is a prediction, and in $BF_3$ it is the chemically most important orbital in the molecule.

9. High Symmetry: $CH_4$ and $SF_6$

$CH_4$ — $T_d$

$$ \Gamma_{\sigma}(4\times\text{H }1s) = A_1 + T_2 $$
  • ● C $2s \to A_1$; C $(2p_x,2p_y,2p_z) \to T_2$. Exact match, no orbital left over.
  • ● Eight valence electrons fill $a_1$ and the triply degenerate $t_2$.
Four equivalent bonds, two different orbital energies. Photoelectron spectroscopy of $CH_4$ shows two ionisations, not one. The $sp^3$ hybrid picture predicts four degenerate bonding orbitals and is simply wrong about this observable. The MO picture predicts $a_1$ and $t_2$ at different energies, which is what is measured. Hybrid orbitals describe the bond directions, never the orbital energies.

$SF_6$ — $O_h$

$$ \Gamma_{\sigma}(6\times\text{F}) = A_{1g} + E_g + T_{1u} $$
  • ● S $3s \to A_{1g}$, S $3p \to T_{1u}$. That accounts for four of the six ligand SALCs.
  • ● The $E_g$ pair has no valence $s$ or $p$ partner on sulfur. Its bonding combination is delocalised over the six F atoms.
No expanded octet is required. The classical account invokes $3d$ participation to reach twelve electrons around sulfur. Modern calculations put $3d$ occupancy near zero. Six bonds arise from four sulfur-based bonding orbitals plus two ligand-based $e_g$ combinations — hypervalency is a delocalisation result, and it is the same $\Gamma_\sigma$ we will reuse for $[ML_6]$ in §10.

10. Walsh Diagrams

A Walsh diagram plots orbital energy against a geometric coordinate — usually a bond angle. It answers a question no single MO diagram can: which shape does the molecule choose?

Only the slope of each line carries information. Absolute energies in a Walsh diagram are not meaningful.

Walsh's rule

The molecule adopts the geometry that minimises the sum of occupied orbital energies. In practice: look at the highest occupied orbital and follow its line. If it falls on bending, the molecule bends.

Why $2a_1$ falls so steeply

In the linear molecule it is a member of the non-bonding $\pi_u$ pair on the central atom. Bending destroys the degeneracy: one component acquires central-atom $s$ character and can now overlap with both H atoms, so it is stabilised. Its partner $1b_1$ stays perpendicular to the plane and is untouched — hence the flat dashed line.

Limits. Walsh diagrams are one-dimensional and qualitative. They order geometries; they do not give bond angles. Where two lines have opposing slopes of similar magnitude — or where the species is a radical — the prediction can fail.

The rule in one line

$AH_2$ with 4 valence electrons is linear; with 5 to 8 it is bent, and the angle tightens as $2a_1$ goes from singly to doubly occupied. Compare the two states of $CH_2$: same molecule, same electron count, 34° apart — because one of them puts a second electron into the steeply falling orbital.

What this buys over VSEPR

VSEPR also predicts bent $H_2O$, and faster. But VSEPR cannot distinguish singlet from triplet $CH_2$, cannot say why the angle tightens, and has nothing to say about the two ionisation energies of $CH_4$ in §9. Orbital symmetry explains the cases where electron-pair repulsion runs out of arguments.

12. Advanced: $O_h$ $\sigma$-bonding

In transition metal complexes (e.g., $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$), we use the 6 ligand lone pair vectors as basis set.

$$ \Gamma_{\sigma} = A_{1g} + E_g + T_{1u} $$

Matching Metal d-orbitals in $O_h$:

  • $s$ orbital: $A_{1g}$ (Match!)
  • $p$ orbitals: $T_{1u}$ (Match!)
  • $d$ orbitals ($d_{z^2}, d_{x^2-y^2}$): $E_g$ (Match!)
  • $d$ orbitals ($d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{zx}$): $T_{2g}$ (Non-Bonding!)

13. Transition to Ligand Field Theory

The splitting of d-orbitals in a complex is a direct result of these symmetry interactions.

$\sigma$-Bonding

Increases the energy of $e_g$ orbitals (Stronger overlap).

$\pi$-Bonding

Affects the $t_{2g}$ orbitals. Can be donor (weak field) or acceptor (strong field).
