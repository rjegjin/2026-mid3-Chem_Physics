Lecture 05: LCAO, Bond Order, and the $s$–$p$ Mixing Problem

0. Why Diatomics Are Worth a Whole Lecture

A diatomic has one bond and two centres. Everything that will later be buried inside a $[\mathrm{ML_6}]$ complex is here, exposed and countable.

What this module settles

  • ● The two conditions under which atomic orbitals combine at all — used unchanged in Modules 06 and 08.
  • ● Bond order, and what it does and does not predict.
  • ● Why the level ordering changes partway across the second row.
  • ● The $\mathrm{CO}$ frontier orbitals — the single most reused result in Modules 11 to 13.
One observation decides the whole argument. $\mathrm{O_2}$ is paramagnetic. Every Lewis structure of $\mathrm{O_2}$ pairs all twelve valence electrons and therefore predicts a diamagnetic molecule. The measurement says otherwise.

This is not a subtlety to note in passing. It is the reason molecular orbital theory replaced the localised picture for these molecules, and §8 is built around it.

Scope

Second-row diatomics, homonuclear and heteronuclear. Symmetry labels are $D_{\infty h}$ and $C_{\infty v}$, but the point-group machinery itself is recovered from Modules 01–02 rather than rebuilt. Quantitative coefficients are not computed; we go as far as ordering, occupancy and bond order.

1. LCAO and Its Two Conditions

$$ \psi_{\pm} = c_A \phi_A \pm c_B \phi_B $$

Two atomic orbitals give two molecular orbitals — never one, never three. Orbitals are conserved in the combination.

Condition 1

Comparable energies

If $\phi_A$ and $\phi_B$ lie far apart in energy, the mixing is negligible and both orbitals stay essentially atomic. This is why $\mathrm{H}$ $1s$ interacts with $\mathrm{F}$ $2p$ and not with $\mathrm{F}$ $1s$.

Condition 2

Matching symmetry

$\langle \phi_A | \hat{H} | \phi_B \rangle$ vanishes identically unless the two belong to the same irreducible representation. Not "small" — exactly zero, by the theorem of Bonus 04.

Why condition 2 is the powerful one

Condition 1 is a matter of degree; condition 2 is absolute. A $\sigma$ orbital and a $\pi$ orbital on the same two atoms, at identical energies, do not interact at all — their overlap integral is zero by symmetry because the positive and negative regions cancel exactly. That single fact is what makes MO diagrams tractable, and it is the whole content of the SALC method in Module 06.

2. The Overlap Integral, and Why Antibonding Is Worse

$$ E_{\pm} = \frac{H_{AA} \pm H_{AB}}{1 \pm S} $$

The asymmetry, in one line

The denominator is $1+S$ for the bonding combination and $1-S$ for the antibonding one. With $S > 0$, the antibonding level is pushed up further than the bonding level is pushed down.

This is why $\mathrm{He_2}$ does not exist. Filling $\sigma$ and $\sigma^*$ equally does not cancel out to zero — it comes out net repulsive. Bond order zero is the bookkeeping; the unequal splitting is the reason.

The same asymmetry explains why a filled–filled interaction between a ligand lone pair and a filled metal orbital is destabilising — a point Module 08 needs for $\pi$ donors.

4. Labels: $\sigma$, $\pi$, $g$, $u$

A homonuclear diatomic belongs to $D_{\infty h}$. Two labels carry all the information we need.

$\sigma$ or $\pi$ — behaviour about the bond axis

  • ● $\sigma$: cylindrically symmetric about the axis. Built from $s$, $p_z$, $d_{z^2}$.
  • ● $\pi$: one nodal plane containing the axis. Built from $p_x, p_y$. Always doubly degenerate — and that degeneracy is what makes $\mathrm{O_2}$ a triplet.

$g$ or $u$ — behaviour under inversion

  • ● $g$ (gerade): unchanged on inverting through the centre.
  • ● $u$ (ungerade): changes sign.
  • ● Bonding $\sigma$ is $\sigma_g$; antibonding $\sigma$ is $\sigma_u^*$. For $\pi$ it is the other way round: bonding $\pi$ is $\pi_u$, antibonding is $\pi_g^*$.
The $\pi$ reversal catches everyone once. It is not a convention to memorise — work it out. A bonding $\pi$ orbital already has a nodal plane through the axis, so inverting through the centre swaps the two lobes and flips the sign: ungerade. Adding the antibonding node restores the symmetry: gerade.

Why bother? Because $g/u$ is exactly what the Laporte rule reads in Module 09, and heteronuclear diatomics have no $g/u$ label at all — $C_{\infty v}$ has no centre of inversion.

5. The $2s$-Only Regime

$\mathrm{H_2}$, $\mathrm{He_2}$, $\mathrm{Li_2}$, $\mathrm{Be_2}$ — the $2p$ orbitals are empty and stay out of it.

$\mathrm{H_2}$: BO 1, diamagnetic, $D_0 = 436$ kJ mol$^{-1}$.

$\mathrm{Be_2}$ is a useful embarrassment. Simple bond-order counting says it does not exist, yet a weakly bound $\mathrm{Be_2}$ is observed with $D_0 \approx 10$ kJ mol$^{-1}$. The binding comes from mixing in the empty $2p$ orbitals, which the naive diagram leaves out. Bond order zero means "no ordinary covalent bond", not "no interaction whatsoever".

$\mathrm{Li_2}$ and $\mathrm{Be_2}$ have $\mathrm{He}$-like cores; the $1s$ pairs are so contracted that they neither overlap nor matter. From here on only valence electrons are counted.

6. $s$–$p$ Mixing Changes the Order

$2s$ and $2p_z$ on the same atom are both $\sigma$ with respect to the bond axis. They share a symmetry label, so by §1 they must mix.

The mechanism

Mixing pushes the lower $\sigma$ (mostly $2s$) further down and the upper $\sigma$ (mostly $2p_z$) further up. The $\pi$ orbitals cannot participate — wrong symmetry — so they stay put. When the push is large enough, $\sigma_g(2p)$ rises above $\pi_u(2p)$.

When it is strong

Mixing scales with how close $2s$ and $2p$ lie. That gap widens across the row as $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ grows, so mixing weakens from B to F. The observed crossover falls between $\mathrm{N_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$.

MoleculeOrder
$\mathrm{B_2}$, $\mathrm{C_2}$, $\mathrm{N_2}$$\pi_u$ below $\sigma_g$

The ordering is not a postulate. It is read off photoelectron spectra, which give the occupied orbital energies directly.

$\mathrm{B_2}$6$\sigma_g^2 \sigma_u^{*2} \pi_u^2$12paramagnetic

$\mathrm{N_2}$10$\sigma_g^2 \sigma_u^{*2} \pi_u^4 \sigma_g^2$30diamagnetic

$\mathrm{B_2}$ is paramagnetic

Two electrons enter the degenerate $\pi_u$ pair and, by Hund's rule, stay unpaired. Observed. It also confirms $\pi_u$ lies below $\sigma_g$ — on the other order the two electrons would pair in $\sigma_g$ and $\mathrm{B_2}$ would be diamagnetic.

$\mathrm{C_2}$ has a $\pi$-only double bond

Its BO of 2 comes from two $\pi$ bonds and no net $\sigma$ — unlike any double bond in organic chemistry. A curiosity, and a reminder that BO counts electrons, not bond types.

$\mathrm{N_2}$ and its HOMO

BO 3 and $D_0 = 945$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ — among the strongest bonds known, which is why nitrogen fixation is hard (Module 15). Its HOMO is $\sigma_g$, weakly antibonding and pointing along the axis.

Hund and Mulliken, 1927–1932. Molecular orbital theory grew alongside Pauling's valence-bond theory, and for two decades the two competed. Robert Mulliken coined the word "orbital" and received the 1966 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the MO approach. The diatomic series was its proving ground, because these are the molecules where the two theories disagree about something measurable.

8. $\mathrm{O_2}$ — The Case That Settled It

Why it comes out this way

Twelve valence electrons.

Ten of them fill everything up to the bonding $\pi$ level. The last two enter the doubly degenerate antibonding $\pi$ pair, and Hund's rule puts them in separate orbitals with parallel spins.

Two unpaired electrons: a triplet ground state, with bond order 2.

What the Lewis structure gets wrong. $\mathrm{O{=}O}$ with two lone pairs on each atom accounts for all twelve electrons in pairs, so it predicts diamagnetism. Liquid oxygen sticks to the poles of a magnet. The structure is not merely incomplete here — it is wrong about an observable.

Module 07 recovered

The spin-only formula $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mu_B$ gives $2.83\,\mu_B$ for $n = 2$ — the same expression, applied to a molecule rather than a metal ion. Degeneracy plus Hund's rule gives unpaired electrons in both settings.

A correction worth making. The paramagnetism of oxygen was measured long before MO theory — Faraday examined it in the 1840s. MO theory did not predict it; it explained it, in Lennard-Jones's 1929 treatment, while the localised picture could not. That is a weaker claim than "prediction" and it is the accurate one.

Read the table twice

Down the BO column: bond length falls and $D_0$ rises with bond order, monotonically, peaking at $\mathrm{N_2}$. Down the "unpaired" column: only $\mathrm{B_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$ are paramagnetic — exactly the two molecules with a partly filled degenerate $\pi$ set.

Why $\mathrm{F_2}$ is so weak. BO 1, yet $D_0$ is only 159 kJ mol$^{-1}$ against 436 for $\mathrm{H_2}$. Bond order cannot see this: fluorine's filled $\pi_u$ and $\pi_g^*$ shells put a great deal of electron density in a small volume, and lone-pair repulsion eats most of the bond energy. This is the clearest single case of what §3 warned about.

10. Heteronuclear Diatomics: the Coefficients Go Unequal

Once the two atoms differ, $|c_A| \ne |c_B|$. Every molecular orbital leans toward one atom, and which way it leans is the whole content of polarity in MO language.

The rule

A molecular orbital resembles whichever atomic orbital it lies closer to in energy. The bonding orbital sits near fluorine and is mostly fluorine; the antibonding orbital sits near hydrogen and is mostly hydrogen. Hence the bonding pair is on fluorine — the polarity, without ever invoking electronegativity as a separate idea.

Non-bonding orbitals appear

$\mathrm{F}$ $2p_x$ and $2p_y$ have $\pi$ symmetry, and hydrogen offers nothing of $\pi$ symmetry to pair with. They stay exactly where they were — genuinely non-bonding, not weakly bonding.

No $g$ or $u$ here. $\mathrm{HF}$ belongs to $C_{\infty v}$, which has no inversion centre, so the labels do not exist. In Module 09 this matters: without a centre of inversion the Laporte rule has nothing to forbid.

11. $\mathrm{CO}$ — the Most Reused Diagram in This Course

$\mathrm{CO}$ is isoelectronic with $\mathrm{N_2}$: ten valence electrons, BO 3, diamagnetic. And yet its chemistry is entirely different, because its orbitals are lopsided.

HOMO — a $\sigma$ orbital weighted on carbon

Oxygen is more electronegative, so one expects everything to lean toward oxygen. For the HOMO it does not. $s$–$p$ mixing (§6) pushes this $\sigma$ level up and concentrates it on the carbon end, where it projects outward as a lone pair.

Consequence: $\mathrm{CO}$ binds metals through carbon. Every metal carbonyl in Modules 11 to 13 is $\mathrm{M{-}C{\equiv}O}$, never $\mathrm{M{-}O{\equiv}C}$.

LUMO — $\pi^*$, also weighted on carbon

The empty $\pi^*$ pair has its larger lobes on carbon too. A metal $d_\pi$ orbital can therefore donate into it from the same side the $\sigma$ donation came from.

That is $\pi$ back-donation, and it is why $\mathrm{CO}$ sits at the strong-field end of the spectrochemical series in Module 08.

$$ \mathrm{CO\ free}:\ \nu_{\mathrm{CO}} = 2143\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}} \qquad \mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}:\ 2057 \qquad \mathrm{[Fe(CO)_4]^{2-}}:\ 1790 $$
Read that row carefully — it is a measurement, not an illustration. Filling the $\pi^*$ orbital weakens the C–O bond, so the stretching frequency falls. The more electron-rich the metal, the further it falls. $\nu_{\mathrm{CO}}$ is therefore a direct experimental readout of electron density at a metal centre, and Module 11 uses it exactly that way.
Zeise's salt, 1827. William Zeise prepared $\mathrm{K[PtCl_3(C_2H_4)]}$ — the first organometallic compound with a $\pi$-bound ligand — and could not say what held it together. The bonding was only explained in 1953 by the Dewar–Chatt–Duncanson model, using the same donation-plus-back-donation logic as above. It took 126 years.

Removing the odd electron from $\pi^*$ shortens the bond by 9 pm and takes $\mathrm{NO^+}$ to the same configuration as $\mathrm{CO}$ and $\mathrm{N_2}$.

Where the electron sits

Eleven valence electrons cannot pair up. The last one occupies a $\pi^*$ orbital, giving BO 2.5 and one unpaired electron. $\mathrm{NO}$ is a stable radical — unusual, and the reason it works as a signalling molecule in biology.

Preview: a ligand whose oxidation state is ambiguous. Bound to a metal, that $\pi^*$ electron can sit largely on the ligand or largely on the metal, so $\mathrm{NO}$ can be counted as $\mathrm{NO^+}$ (linear M–N–O) or $\mathrm{NO^-}$ (bent). The electron-counting methods of Module 11 give different oxidation states depending on the choice — the standard example of a non-innocent ligand.

The M–N–O angle is measurable, so the ambiguity is resolved case by case in the structure rather than by convention.
